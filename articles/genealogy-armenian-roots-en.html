Between these two dates, and several equally pivotal ones that followed — the massacres and genocide of 1915, the Soviet repatriation of 1946–1948 — lies the history of the Armenian presence in Russia, which turns out on closer inspection to be not one but several largely independent communities: the Don community, the Astrakhan community, the Moscow community, the Transcaucasian community. Each has left its own archival trail, and before searching for documents, it's worth working out which of these histories belongs to your own family.

Not one people in one place: the Armenian communities of the empire

The mistake that leads many searches astray is treating "Armenian roots in Russia" as a single story. In reality, this covers at least four largely independent communities, each with its own geography, founding period, and archival logic. The oldest is the trading diaspora of Astrakhan and the lower Volga, formed as early as the 17th century around transit trade between Persia and Europe. The second is the Don Armenians, resettlers from Crimea in 1779–1780, who founded Nakhichevan-on-Don and five surrounding villages. The third is the Transcaucasian Armenians, whose ancestors had lived for centuries on lands that became part of the empire after 1801–1828, following the annexation of Eastern Georgia and the Erivan and Nakhichevan khanates. The fourth is the refugees and migrants of the late 19th and early 20th centuries, fleeing massacres in the Ottoman Empire and, above all, the genocide of 1915.

A geographic and chronological marker almost always points to the right direction of search: the Don Armenians lived compactly around Rostov and spoke a Crimean dialect of Armenian with noticeable Turkic borrowings (a legacy of centuries of life in Crimea); the Transcaucasian Armenians were the indigenous population of their lands and did not migrate en masse during the imperial period; refugees of the 1890s–1920s settled wherever they could — from the Kuban to Moscow and St. Petersburg — and almost always left a documentary trail specifically as refugees rather than as longstanding imperial subjects.

Nakhichevan-on-Don: the Crimean exodus and a city no longer on the map

The resettlement of Crimean Armenians in 1779–1780 was organized in parallel with, and nearly simultaneously to, the resettlement of Crimean Greeks — both operations were overseen by Grigory Potemkin and carried out by General Suvorov, and both pursued the same goal: to strip the Crimean Khanate of its Christian, taxpaying population. The spiritual leader of the resettlement was Archbishop Hovsep Argutinsky, who later became Catholicos of All Armenians; his role in persuading the flock was so significant that in the Don community his memory was honored on a par with the town's founders.

About 12,600 Armenians, who endured the same grueling winter crossing as the Greeks, founded the town of Nakhichevan on the bank of the Don in 1780, along with five villages — Chaltyr, Krym, Bolshiye Saly, Sultan-Saly, and Nesvetay — whose names remain today the best clue to an Armenian trace in the region: if an ancestor is recorded as a native of one of these settlements in Rostov district, this almost certainly points to a descendant of the Crimean resettlers. Like the Mariupol Greeks, the Don Armenians received a special estate autonomy — the Nakhichevan Armenian Magistrate, in operation until the 1870s, which kept its own family lists and household records separately from the general Don Cossack administration. In 1928, the Soviet government annexed Nakhichevan to Rostov-on-Don as a city district, and today the town survives as a separate administrative unit only in archival documents and old maps.

Astrakhan and Moscow Armenians: a trading diaspora older than the empire itself

Long before the Crimean resettlement, a thriving Armenian merchant colony already existed on the Volga. Astrakhan, as a key transit port between Persia, India, and Europe, attracted Armenian traders from the mid-17th century onward; in 1667, an Armenian trading company from New Julfa (a suburb of Persian Isfahan) received trade privileges from Tsar Alexei Mikhailovich, securing Armenian merchants a special status along the entire Volga trade route. By the 18th century, a distinct Armenian quarter had formed in Astrakhan with its own church and school, whose records are held today at the State Archive of Astrakhan Oblast.

The Moscow Armenian community, considerably smaller but highly influential, formed around several merchant families, the most important of which was the Lazarev (Lazarian) family — originally from New Julfa, who grew wealthy in the silk trade and later founded the renowned Lazarev Institute of Oriental Languages in Moscow in 1815. The Lazarev family archive, including extensive correspondence and household records, is today split between the Russian State Archive of Ancient Acts (RGADA) and the Manuscripts Department of the Russian State Library.

1828: Eastern Armenia becomes Russian

The Treaty of Turkmenchay, which ended the Russo-Persian War of 1826–1828, transferred to Russia the Erivan and Nakhichevan khanates — the territory of historic Eastern Armenia, which had for centuries been under Persian rule. The treaty provided not only for territorial transfer but also for organized resettlement: tens of thousands of Armenians living on the Persian side of the new border were given the right, and every assistance, to move within Russian borders. The resettlement was organized in part by the writer and diplomat Alexander Griboyedov, then Russia's envoy to Persia — a tragic irony, since it was precisely for defending the interests of Armenian resettlers against Persian anger that he paid with his life in 1829, when the Russian mission in Tehran was overrun.

A similar, though less organized, process unfolded on the Ottoman side: after each 19th-century Russo-Turkish war (1828–1829, 1853–1856, 1877–1878), new groups of Western Armenians crossed into Russian Transcaucasia, fleeing unrest in the eastern vilayets of the Ottoman Empire. Unlike the Don Armenians, the Transcaucasian resettlers received no separate estate self-government — they fell under the general administrative system of Erivan province and, later, the Kars region, which makes their archival trace less distinct but at the same time more numerous and geographically concentrated.

The Armenian Apostolic Church and its archival system

A key structural feature of Armenian genealogy in the Russian Empire is a separate church, independent of both the Orthodox and any other confessional system. The Armenian Apostolic Church answered not to the Holy Synod but to the Catholicosate of Etchmiadzin, the spiritual center of all Armenians, located in Etchmiadzin (now the city of Vagharshapat in Armenia). After the annexation of Eastern Armenia, Etchmiadzin found itself within the empire, giving Russian Armenians a unique advantage: their parish registers were kept under a single church-wide system rather than scattered among local consistories.

In practice, this means that parish registers of Armenian congregations — from Don Nakhichevan to the Baku oilfields — should be sought not in local provincial archives but in church archival collections: a significant portion of such documents is concentrated today at the National Archives of Armenia in Yerevan, where materials from diocesan archives across Transcaucasia flowed after incorporation into the USSR, as well as at Etchmiadzin itself, which retains part of its historical records in the Catholicosate's own archive.

1915: genocide and a wave of refugees into Russia

The Armenian genocide in the Ottoman Empire, which began in April 1915 and claimed, by various estimates, between 600,000 and one and a half million lives, produced the largest wave of Armenian migration into Russian territory in the entire imperial period. Western Armenians fleeing the massacres crossed the Caucasus border by the tens and hundreds of thousands, settling primarily in Russian Transcaucasia but also in the North Caucasus, the Kuban, Rostov, and even Moscow and St. Petersburg. A significant share of the refugees never returned to their historic homeland, remaining in the USSR and forming an additional layer of Armenian population on top of the already-existing Don and Transcaucasian communities.

For genealogical research, this wave leaves a distinctive trail: refugee registration lists, documents of charitable committees for Armenian relief (active in Tiflis, Rostov, and other cities), and census materials on refugees compiled first by the Russian and later by the Soviet state. Part of these documents survives in regional archives of the North Caucasus and Transcaucasia, part in the collections of international humanitarian organizations that worked with Armenian refugees between 1915 and 1923.

The 1946–1948 Soviet repatriation: a reverse flow

After World War II, the Soviet government organized a large-scale campaign to repatriate ethnic Armenians of the diaspora to the Armenian SSR — between 1946 and 1948, around 100,000 people resettled in the republic from Iran, Lebanon, Syria, France, Greece, the United States, and other countries where a substantial Armenian diaspora had formed after the 1915 genocide. The campaign was presented as a reunification of the nation with its historic homeland, but in practice it combined the genuine patriotic impulse of many repatriates with strict Soviet control over their subsequent lives — some resettlers later faced additional restrictions and surveillance as "politically unreliable" due to their foreign origin.

For families whose history is tied to this wave, the key documents are the repatriate case files kept by Soviet migration and registration authorities, as well as the archives of Armenian diaspora organizations in the countries of origin (Lebanon, Syria, France), which often hold far more detailed prewar records of these families than the surviving Soviet sources.

Practical archives and sources

For Don Armenians, the starting point is the State Archive of Rostov Oblast, which holds the collections of the former Nakhichevan Armenian Magistrate: family lists, revision tallies, and household and judicial case files. For the Astrakhan merchant community, the State Archive of Astrakhan Oblast is the place to turn, preserving documents of the Armenian quarter and its associated parishes.

For Transcaucasian Armenians, the main body of documents is concentrated at the National Archives of Armenia in Yerevan, which includes both civil and church collections; a significant share of materials on the western districts that became part of the Kars region also turns up in Georgian archival collections, owing to the shared administrative structure of the Caucasus Viceroyalty. A separate and highly valuable resource is the Matenadaran, the Institute of Ancient Manuscripts in Yerevan: although its collection focuses on medieval manuscripts rather than family records, it also carries out work systematizing later diaspora archival materials.

For families affected by the 1915 genocide, a key source is the archive of the Armenian Genocide Museum-Institute in Yerevan, which has gathered tens of thousands of eyewitness testimonies and refugee lists, along with the international Houshamadyan project, dedicated to reconstructing the history of Western Armenian villages and towns destroyed or depopulated by the genocide — its database covers place names, surnames, and oral histories often found in no official archive.

Where to start: a step-by-step plan

The first step is to identify which of the four communities the family belongs to, based on geography (the Don and southern Russia versus Transcaucasia versus Astrakhan and the Volga), the surname of the settlement in old documents (Chaltyr, Krym, Bolshiye Saly are a reliable sign of the Don line), and family lore about the move (sudden flight from massacre is a marker of the 1915 refugee wave, while calm, centuries-long settlement points to Transcaucasian roots).

The second step — for the Don line — is to contact the Rostov Oblast Archive for documents of the former Nakhichevan Magistrate; for the Transcaucasian line, begin the search directly at the National Archives of Armenia, bypassing general Russian catalogs. The third step — for any suspected connection to the 1915 genocide — is to check the database of the Armenian Genocide Museum-Institute and the Houshamadyan project, which can reconstruct a family's history down to a specific Western Armenian village, even where Soviet documents break off at the status of "refugee." The fourth step, relevant for families of the postwar repatriation, is to request the repatriate's case file through Armenia's migration service archives, and in parallel to contact diaspora organizations in the country of origin, which often hold more complete prewar records than Soviet sources.

Conclusion

Armenian genealogy in Russia is unusual in that it groups under a single ethnonym no fewer than four histories, each with a different archival research logic: the state-organized Crimean resettlement to the Don, the centuries-old merchant diaspora of Astrakhan and Moscow, the indigenous Transcaucasian population that joined the empire along with its land, and the tragic refugee wave of the 1915 genocide. To this is added a mirror-image movement, the postwar repatriation, which turned part of the diaspora back toward Soviet Armenia.

The practical takeaway for a researcher: first identify your branch by geography, the surname of the ancestral village, and family lore about the nature of the move, then go directly to the relevant archive — Don, Astrakhan, or Yerevan — without wasting time on general provincial catalogs, which for the empire's Armenian communities almost always turn out to be a supplementary rather than a primary source.

Glossary

Nakhichevan Armenian Magistrate — the estate self-government body of the Don Armenians, in operation from the 1780s to the 1870s.

Catholicosate of Etchmiadzin — the spiritual and administrative center of the Armenian Apostolic Church, which unified the parish registers of Armenian congregations across the empire.

Treaty of Turkmenchay — the 1828 agreement under which Eastern Armenia (the Erivan and Nakhichevan khanates) became part of the Russian Empire.

Matenadaran — the Institute of Ancient Manuscripts in Yerevan, the world's largest collection of Armenian manuscripts.

The 1946–1948 repatriation — the Soviet state's organized campaign to resettle ethnic Armenians of the diaspora in the Armenian SSR.

Houshamadyan — an international research project documenting the history of Western Armenian (Ottoman) villages and towns destroyed or depopulated by the 1915 genocide.
