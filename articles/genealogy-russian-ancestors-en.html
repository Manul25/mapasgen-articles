§ 01

In 1666–1667, a church council met in Moscow and, for the first time in Russia, formally raised the question of keeping records of parishioners' births, marriages, and deaths. The matter, however, stayed mostly talk for a long while: in 1702, Peter the Great issued a separate decree requiring Moscow priests to submit weekly reports on births and deaths to the Patriarchal Spiritual Office, but even that never became standard practice. It wasn't until May 1722, in an addendum to the Spiritual Regulation, that a requirement finally took hold: every parish in the empire was now obliged to keep parish registers. Modern genealogists treat this date as a convenient starting point — from 1722 onward, written traces of the ancestors of people who lived in the Russian Empire become genuinely abundant, regardless of which people, social class, or faith a given family belonged to.

More than three centuries have passed since then, along with two changes of political system, several wars, and waves of emigration — and today, documents about one's ancestors can be sitting almost anywhere: in a regional archive covering a former province, in the holdings of a central Moscow archive, on a microfilm shot by Mormon missionaries during the Soviet era, or in the police prefecture of a French town where an émigré fleeing the revolution once settled. This article is a detailed, practical breakdown of how this whole system of sources works, what documents exist for different eras and social groups, and who to contact at each stage of the search.

§ 02

Start with Conversations in the Family

Before storming the archives, it's worth exhausting the most accessible source of all: the memory of living relatives. The names of great-grandparents, village names, occupations, stories about moves, old letters, photographs with captions on the back, military service records, marriage certificates — all of this sharply narrows down the archival search. Soviet-era documents are especially valuable, since they often state a place of birth, which immediately points to which regional archive might hold earlier parish records. Don't dismiss oral details that seem trivial at first glance, either: a remark like "our family was all from somewhere near Tula" or "grandfather said he was born into an Old Believer family" is already a search direction and a hint at which type of document you'll need.

§ 03

Parish Registers: The Main Source Before 1918

A parish register (metricheskaya kniga) was a document kept in every Orthodox parish, divided into three sections: births, marriages, and deaths. A birth entry typically recorded the date of birth and baptism, the infant's name, the names and social status of the parents, and the names of godparents — who were often relatives or close neighbours of the family and can themselves serve as a good lead for further research. A marriage entry included the names and ages of the bride and groom, their social class, which marriage this was for each of them (first, second, and so on), and the names of witnesses. A death entry recorded the deceased's age, cause of death (often imprecise, phrased as "of fever" or "of old age"), and place of burial.

The registers were kept in two copies: one stayed at the church itself, the other was copied out each year and sent for safekeeping to the consistory — the administrative body of the diocese. This matters in practice: if a particular church's parish copy hasn't survived (and after the revolution, wars, and simple time, a great deal hasn't), there's a chance the consistory copy survived — and the reverse is also true: sometimes only the parish copy made it through.

Non-Orthodox peoples of the empire had their own arrangements. Old Believers only gained the right to keep their own registers after an 1905 law — before that, they were registered by secular district administrations. Records for Muslims were kept by mullahs at mosques, for Jews by rabbis or specially appointed community scribes, and for Lutherans by pastors, and weren't always written in Russian. If your ancestors belonged to one of these groups, you'll need to look not only in consistory holdings but also in district administration records, the registers of the relevant religious community, or, for Jews of the Russian Empire, in specialized databases such as JewishGen, which gather precisely these records in one place.

Good news for anyone starting a search today: a substantial share of parish registers has already been digitized. In the mid-twentieth century, Mormon missions carried out large-scale microfilming of archives worldwide, including Soviet ones, and today that material is freely available on FamilySearch. You can check the survival catalogue for registers held in Russian, Ukrainian, and Belarusian archives on the specialized site metrics.tilda.ws, while the originals generally remain at the regional state archive covering the province where your ancestors lived — and modern archives are increasingly posting scans in their own information systems.

§ 04

Confession Lists: A Backup Source When Registers Are Missing

Besides parish registers, churches also drew up another annual document: the confession list, or ispovednaya rospis'. A standard format for these was introduced by a Synod decree in 1737 and remained almost unchanged until 1917, with some places continuing to compile them even into the 1920s. Unlike a parish register, which records individual events, a confession list is essentially an annual census of every parishioner in the entire parish, organized by household and family, noting who confessed and took communion that year and who didn't, and why. For a genealogist, this is an invaluable source: you can see a family's full composition in a given year even if none of its members happened to be born or die that particular year — and so wouldn't appear in the parish register at all. Confession lists have survived less well, on average, than parish registers, but that's exactly why it's worth checking both sources in parallel: what's missing from one sometimes turns up in the other.

§ 05

Revision Lists and Censuses: Records Kept for Tax Purposes

Alongside church record-keeping, Russia ran a separate, secular system of accounting from 1718 onward: the revisions, or censuses of the taxable population for tax purposes. The unit of account was the "revision soul," and information on each household was entered into a document called a revision list (revizskaya skazka): the name, patronymic, and surname of the head of household, the age and composition of the family, each member's relation to the head, and — before the abolition of serfdom — which landowner the peasants belonged to. Between revisions, notes were added on the fate of those absent: died, fled, resettled, drafted as a soldier — entries that often serve as the only clue to what happened to a person afterward. Ten revisions took place in total between 1718 and 1858, and records from the earliest ones are held at the Russian State Archive of Ancient Acts in Moscow.

Revision lists are especially important for those whose ancestors weren't part of Orthodox church record-keeping at all — Muslims, pagans, members of smaller peoples of the empire — and also for serfs, whose attachment to a specific landowner often provides an additional lead: estate archives and the correspondence of landowning families sometimes preserve information about peasant households more precisely than state archives do. In addition, Russia conducted its first general population census in 1897 — modern in structure, with an individual census form for every person, recording age, literacy, occupation, and religion. Unfortunately, the survival of 1897 census forms varies enormously by region: some provinces' records survived almost intact, others only in fragments, but wherever they did survive, this is one of the richest sources available.

§ 06

An Important Practical Detail: Search by Province, Not by the Modern Map

One of the most common mistakes beginning researchers make is trying to locate an archive based on a "modern address." The administrative structure of the Russian Empire was organized into provinces (guberniyas) and districts (uyezds), and their borders almost never match those of today's regions and districts in Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, or the Baltic states. The same settlement might have changed province, then district, and then country over its history, and archival holdings were organized precisely around those pre-revolutionary administrative units — so that's exactly how you need to search.

Specialized mapping services that solve exactly this problem are a huge help here: they overlay old provincial maps onto a modern satellite or schematic map, letting you see which province and district a given point on today's map corresponds to — and, conversely, find a village on an old map that's known today by a different name or no longer exists at all. The best-known and most convenient such resource in the Russian-speaking world is EtoMesto.ru: you can search for settlements by name (including vanished ones), adjust the transparency of the old map laid over the modern one, and flip through dozens of historical maps from different eras — from eighteenth-century general land-survey plans and the mid-nineteenth-century Schubert and Mende maps to Soviet-era military topographic maps from the General Staff. Retromap.ru serves a similar function and is especially convenient for comparison: you can open an old map and a modern one side by side, in two synchronized windows, and study the same territory across different eras at once. Before writing to an archive, it's worth first opening one of these services, locating the village in question on the old map, and pinpointing exactly which consistory's or district's holdings you need to request.

Revision list (ревизская сказка)
A census document from the Russian Empire recording the population at a specific point in time for tax and military conscription purposes.
Confession list
A record confirming that a person has received confession and communion in a given year, serving as proof of religious status and financial reliability.

The world's largest genealogical database, operated by The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, containing billions of ancestral records from around the globe.

The State Archive of the Russian Federation, a repository of historical documents including records of officials, administrative decisions, and state institutions.
Province (губерния)
An administrative division of the Russian Empire roughly equivalent to a region or state, governed by a governor (gubernator).
Volost
The lowest administrative division in the Russian Empire and Soviet Union, comprising several villages, administered by a volost elder or council.
