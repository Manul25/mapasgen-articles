Tracing Greek Roots in Russia: Pontic Greeks and Azov (Mariupol) Greeks

§ 01

On May 18, 1778, an unusual caravan left Crimea: escorted by Russian troops, nearly 18,000 Christians — Greeks, Armenians, Georgians — left the peninsula, persuaded by Metropolitan Ignatius of Gothia and Kaffa to switch allegiance to Russia and abandon their homeland. The stated reason was protecting fellow Christians from oppression by the Crimean khan; the real one was a purely economic calculation in St. Petersburg: stripping the Crimean Khanate of its Christian taxpayers to hasten its collapse. Less than a century later, in an entirely different part of the empire, wagons rolled in the opposite direction — Pontic Greeks were leaving the war-ravaged Ottoman vilayets of Trebizond and Kars in search of refuge on the Caucasian shore of the very same empire that had once carried their Crimean coreligionists to the Azov Sea.

This is how the double, and not always obvious, structure of "Russia's Greeks" took shape: on one side, the Azov, or Mariupol, community — descendants of the 1778 Crimean resettlers, with a century and a half of their own self-government; on the other, the Pontic Greeks of the Caucasus, whose ancestors moved to the empire voluntarily and considerably later, fleeing Ottoman instability. These two groups have different archives, different everyday languages, and a different research logic — and before opening any catalog, it's worth working out which history is closer to your own family.

Azov and Pontic Greeks: two different peoples under one name

§ 02

The mistake that most often leads a search into a dead end is treating "Greek roots in Russia" as a single migration wave. In reality, this covers at least two largely unconnected histories. The Azov Greeks are the resettlers of 1778 and their descendants, concentrated around the city of Mariupol, which they founded, and some twenty surrounding villages. The Pontic Greeks came from the southern and southeastern shore of the Black Sea (the historic region of Pontus, now northeastern Turkey), moving into the Russian Empire throughout most of the 19th century in separate waves, most often following each Russo-Turkish war, and settling not on the Azov but in the Caucasus — in Abkhazia, around Batumi, in the Kars region, and in Stavropol territory.

A language marker helps orient quickly: a significant part of the Azov Greek community spoke not Greek but Urum — a Turkic language of Crimean Tatar origin, preserved precisely because part of Crimea's Christians had already been Turkic-speaking before the resettlement. The other part of the community spoke Rumeic, a dialect of Greek. The Pontic Greeks of the Caucasus, by contrast, almost universally preserved the Pontic dialect of Greek. If family lore includes "Greeks who didn't speak Greek," that's almost certainly a trace of the Azov Urum branch.

The 1778 resettlement: how Metropolitan Ignatius led Christians out of Crimea

§ 03

The idea of resettlement did not originate with the Greeks themselves but with Grigory Potemkin, president of the War College, and Alexander Suvorov, commander of Russian troops in Crimea, whose hands carried out the operation. Metropolitan Ignatius, head of Crimea's Christian flock, became the key figure of persuasion: he was promised continued spiritual authority over the resettlers and special status for the community in its new home. The resettlement encompassed not only ethnic Greeks but also Crimean Armenians, Georgians, and Vlachs, though it was the Greeks, as the largest group, whom history subsequently gave their name to the entire migration.

The journey across the starving steppe through the harsh winter of 1778–1779 proved brutal: by various estimates, a quarter to a third of the resettlers did not survive the first year. In 1780, on the site of a former Zaporozhian Cossack post at the mouth of the Kalmius River on the Sea of Azov, the city of Mariupol was founded, and around it, 20 villages named after the Crimean settlements their founders had come from: Yalta, Urzuf, Sartana, Bugas, Laspi, Manhush, and others. This double, Crimean-Azov naming is a reliable marker: if an ancestor is recorded as a native of "Yalta" or "Urzuf" in Mariupol district of Yekaterinoslav province, this unambiguously points to a descendant of the Crimean resettlers, not a resident of the actual southern coast of Crimea.

The Mariupol Greek self-government: a unique documentary source

§ 04

In gratitude for the hardships of resettlement, and as part of Catherine's broader policy of attracting colonists, the Greeks of the Azov region were granted a rare privilege: their own estate self-government, independent of the general provincial administration. The Mariupol Greek Court, established in 1780, handled not only religious but also judicial, tax, and household affairs of the community until 1859, when the self-government was abolished and the Greeks were equalized in rights and obligations with the rest of the province's population.

For genealogists, this means nearly eighty years of documents kept not by the general provincial chancery but by the community itself: family lists, revision tallies (revizskie skazki) for Greeks kept separately from the rest of the district's population, and judicial and household case files. The bulk of these documents is held today at the State Archive of Donetsk Oblast, with some duplicate materials in Dnipropetrovsk and central Ukrainian archives. Parish registers were kept not by a separate Greek consistory (unlike, say, German Lutherans) but by the Orthodox parishes of the villages themselves — meaning, broadly speaking, the same archival collections as for the surrounding Orthodox population, but geographically tied specifically to the Greek-speaking villages.

Pontic Greeks of the Caucasus: a different migration, different causes

§ 05

While the Azov Greeks left Crimea on the empire's initiative, the Pontic Greeks came to Russia of their own will and gradually, starting at the end of the 18th century and especially after each successive Russo-Turkish war — 1828–1829, 1853–1856, and above all 1877–1878, when Russian Transcaucasia annexed the Kars region along with its already sizable Greek population, and new settlers from the Turkish side of the border followed the troops, fleeing repression and economic ruin in their home vilayets.

Pontic Greeks settled in compact enclaves along the Black Sea coast of the Caucasus — around Sukhumi, Batumi, and Novorossiysk, as well as in the mountainous districts of present-day Georgia, chiefly the Tsalka district, to which settlers from Turkish Gümüşhane were relocated as early as the 1830s. A separate and especially large group settled in the Kars region, part of Russia from 1878 to 1918 — today Turkish territory, which creates an additional complication for genealogists: pre-revolutionary documents on the Kars region are held partly in Russian, partly in Georgian, and partly in Turkish archives, with a portion simply lost when the region was transferred to Turkey after the First World War.

Unlike the Azov Greeks, the Pontic Greeks had no separate estate self-government — they were registered as ordinary subjects of the relevant Russian provinces and territories, which simplifies the search in one sense (there's no need to look for a specific estate archive) and complicates it in another: their parish registers are intermixed with the general body of church documents of the Caucasus Viceroyalty, without being separated into a distinct collection.

§ 06

The Soviet period: the NKVD's "Greek operation" and the 1949 deportation

The 20th century treated both Greek communities of the empire equally harshly, though at different times. In 1937–1938, as part of the so-called Greek operation of the NKVD — one of the "national operations" of the Great Terror — an estimated 20,000 to 50,000 Soviet Greeks were arrested on charges of spying for Greece; a large share of those arrested were executed, the rest sent to labor camps. Both the Azov and Pontic Greek communities suffered, but the Black Sea coast of the Caucasus was hit especially hard, since by that point it had the most visible, and therefore most vulnerable, Greek enclaves.

The second blow came in the postwar years: in June 1949, Operation Volna deported the Greek population of Crimea, the Black Sea coast of the Caucasus, and Georgia to Kazakhstan and Central Asia — formally on charges of collaborating with the occupiers during the war, in fact as a continuation of the broader postwar policy of ethnic cleansing along the Black Sea coast. For family research, this means that 1940s documents often break off abruptly: the last mention of a family at its prewar residence, and the first already in NKVD special-settlement records in Kazakhstan, with no smooth archival transition between the two.

§ 07

Practical archives and sources

For Azov Greeks, the starting point is the State Archive of Donetsk Oblast, where the bulk of the former Mariupol Greek District's documents are concentrated: family lists, revision tallies, and parish registers of the Greek-speaking villages. A significant portion of materials also turns up in the collections of the Yekaterinoslav Ecclesiastical Consistory, since, from a religious standpoint, the Azov Greeks were part of the general Orthodox diocesan structure despite their separate estate self-government.

For the Pontic Greeks of the Caucasus, the search is complicated by regional fragmentation: parish registers of Transcaucasian congregations from the late 19th to early 20th century should be sought at the National Archives of Georgia in Tbilisi (for the Tsalka and Batumi areas) and in regional archives of Krasnodar Krai (for the Black Sea coast). For the Kars region, a substantial share of pre-1918 materials ended up in Russian military-historical archives due to the region's military administration, while another portion is held in Georgian collections following the region's incorporation into the Transcaucasian republic in 1918.

§ 08

A separate category of sources concerns repression and deportation: rehabilitation case files from the 1990s, which contain brief biographical notes with prewar residence, and NKVD special-settler registration cards, held in regional archives of Kazakhstan and in the internal-affairs information centers of the Central Asian republics; for many families, this is the only document linking prewar and postwar family history. A useful supplement comes from Pontic cultural societies — for example, the archives of Pontic Greek associations in Greece (where a significant share of Soviet repatriates settled after the 1990s), which conducted their own collection of oral testimony and genealogical records from emigrants.

Where to start: a step-by-step plan

The first step is to identify which of the two branches your family belongs to, based on geography (the Azov region and southern Ukraine versus the Caucasus and Transcaucasia), the language of family lore (Urum/Turkic versus the Pontic dialect of Greek), and the village name in old documents (a Crimean name in Mariupol district is a reliable marker of the Azov line). The second step — for the Azov line — is to contact the State Archive of Donetsk Oblast directly for family lists and revision tallies; for the Pontic line, start with 20th-century Soviet documents (passports, military IDs), which usually record the exact prewar place of residence and point to which regional archive of the Caucasus or Transcaucasia to search for earlier parish registers.

Rumeics — the Greek-speaking part of the Azov Greek community, who spoke the Rumeic dialect of Greek.

Mariupol Greek District (Greek Court) — the estate self-government body of the Azov Greeks, in operation from 1780 to 1859.

Pontus — a historic region on the southern and southeastern shore of the Black Sea, the origin of the Pontic Greeks; today northeastern Turkey.

The NKVD Greek operation — the 1937–1938 repressive campaign against Soviet Greeks on charges of spying for Greece.

Operation Volna — the June 1949 deportation of the Greek population of Crimea, the Black Sea coast of the Caucasus, and Georgia.

Conclusion

Greek genealogy in Russia is a rare case where a single ethnic label actually conceals two almost non-overlapping histories: the state-organized resettlement of the Crimean flock to the Azov Sea in 1778, and the slow, voluntary migration of Pontic communities to the Caucasus over the following century. Both lines share one thing — the tragic rupture of the 20th century, which struck both communities with the terror of 1937–1938 and the deportation of 1949, which is why the archival trail breaks off at exactly that line for so many families.

The practical takeaway for a researcher is simple: first identify your branch by geography and language, then go not from the general to the specific but directly to the relevant regional archive — Donetsk for the Azov region, Georgian or Krasnodar for the Caucasus — and always check the repression records of the 1930s–1940s, which for the empire's Greek families almost always turn out to be the missing link between pre-revolutionary and modern family history.

§ 09

The third step — for any suspicion of the 1937–1938 repressions or the 1949 deportation — is to request the investigative case file through the regional FSB office or its equivalent, and to check special-settler registration cards in Kazakhstan's archives: these documents often restore biographical details lost from the main archival record. The fourth step, especially productive for the Pontic line, is to reach out to Pontic Greek cultural societies, chiefly Greek ones, which for decades have collected oral histories from repatriates and hold informal but detailed genealogical records that exist in no state catalog.

Glossary

Urums — Turkic-speaking Orthodox Greeks, part of the Azov Greek community, who preserved a Turkic language dating back to their time in Crimea.
