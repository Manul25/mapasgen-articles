Egg Donation and Surrogacy in the Czech Republic: What to Know Before You Start

In 1825, a young Czech physiologist named Jan Evangelista Purkyně, working in Prague, noticed something under his microscope that nobody had paid much attention to before: inside a bird's egg cell, he spotted a large round structure he named the germinal vesicle. Today we know that structure as the nucleus of the egg cell — and Purkyně's discovery was one of the first real steps toward understanding what actually happens inside a female reproductive cell at the very earliest stages of life. Purkyně would go on to make several other famous discoveries — Purkinje cells in the cerebellum, sweat glands — but this early work with a bird's egg became one of the founding bricks of reproductive biology.

Two hundred years later, Purkyně's home country found itself at the centre of a very different but thematically connected story — it became one of the most sought-after destinations in Europe for people looking for a donor egg or considering surrogacy. Let's look at why that happened and what these procedures actually look like in practice in the Czech Republic.

§ 01

Why the Czech Republic Became a Fertility Treatment Hub

There are several genuinely practical reasons behind this. The Czech Republic is part of the Schengen Area, so getting there from most European countries takes only a few hours, with no visa hassle. The country has more than thirty IVF clinics, many of which serve foreign patients in several languages and have well-established logistics — from the first video consultation to coordinating flights around the dates a treatment protocol requires. And, of course, price matters: treatment in the Czech Republic costs noticeably less than in the UK, Germany, or France, at a comparable standard of medical care.

§ 02

Egg Donation in the Czech Republic: The Legal Framework

Egg donation in the Czech Republic is legal, tightly regulated, and built on mutual anonymity: neither the recipient nor the future child will ever learn the donor's identity, and the donor receives no information about who ends up with her eggs. The model is officially altruistic — donors are reimbursed for expenses related to the procedure rather than paid a fee as such, though in practice the level of that reimbursement in the Czech Republic is among the most substantial in Europe. Donors are women aged 18 to 35 who go through medical and psychological screening broadly similar to what's standard elsewhere in the EU.

§ 03

How Egg Donor Screening Actually Works

Before a candidate enters a clinic's donor pool, she goes through several stages of vetting. First comes a general medical work-up and an ovarian reserve assessment — AMH levels and an antral follicle count, the same tests we covered in an earlier article — since clinics prefer donors with a reliably strong response to stimulation, so a single cycle yields enough eggs. Next is expanded genetic carrier screening for common hereditary conditions, a karyotype, and testing for sexually transmitted infections. A psychological interview assesses how deliberately the woman is making the decision to donate and whether she fully understands its irreversible nature. Only after clearing every stage does a donor enter the active pool, from which recipients are matched by blood type, Rh factor, and typically the main phenotypic traits — hair colour, eye colour, build.

For recipients, this means that by the time a specific donor is offered to them, she has already gone through a fairly rigorous selection process — one of the reasons patients from countries with a thinner or less formalized donor pool are drawn to the Czech Republic.

§ 04

Surrogacy: A Legal Grey Zone

The situation with surrogacy is far less clear-cut. The Czech Republic has no dedicated law directly governing surrogacy — it isn't banned, but it also isn't recognized as a distinct legal institution. Under current family law, the mother of a child is always the woman who gave birth to it, regardless of whose egg was used at conception. That means an agreement with a surrogate has no direct legal force: formally, she remains the child's legal mother until further legal steps are completed.

Only the altruistic form of surrogacy is permitted: a surrogate can be reimbursed for actual expenses tied to the pregnancy and birth, but not paid a fee as such. Commercial surrogacy — payment beyond covering expenses — can, in formal terms, be interpreted as the criminal offence of handing over a child for payment. In 2024 and 2025, draft bills were introduced in the Czech parliament aiming to finally establish a clear legal status for surrogacy, but for now it continues to exist in a state of "not banned, but not protected by law either."

§ 05

Who Becomes a Surrogate, and How Matching Works

Only gestational surrogacy is permitted in the Czech Republic: the surrogate carries an embryo that is genetically entirely unrelated to her — created from the intended parents' egg and sperm, or using donor cells. Traditional surrogacy, where the surrogate is also the child's genetic mother, isn't practised in the Czech context precisely because of the additional legal and ethical complications it would create.

Agencies and clinics that work with international patients typically maintain a pool of pre-screened surrogates — women who already have children of their own (which lowers health risks and confirms an ability to carry a pregnancy to term), and who go through medical and psychological evaluation along with legal counsel before the programme begins. Thanks to that existing pool, matching with a suitable surrogate, according to specialist agencies, usually takes anywhere from a few weeks to a couple of months — noticeably faster than in countries without this kind of established infrastructure.

Many Czech programmes combine both elements — egg donation and surrogacy — into a single package, particularly for couples where the intended mother has medical contraindications to both her own eggs and to carrying a pregnancy. In that case, the child is genetically linked to the egg donor and, typically, to the father (if his sperm is used), while the surrogate carrying the pregnancy has no genetic connection to the child at all.

§ 06

The Path to Parental Rights After Birth

Since the surrogate is the default legal mother, the genetic (intended) parents need to go through an additional legal process to establish their own parental rights. This typically works as follows: the intended father acknowledges paternity (a relatively straightforward step, especially if he's the child's genetic father), and the other intended parent then goes through an adoption process to gain parental rights over the child the surrogate carried. According to specialist agencies, the whole journey — from matching with a surrogate to completing every legal formality — typically takes around 16 to 18 months.

§ 07

What It Costs

The cost of a surrogacy programme in the Czech Republic varies noticeably depending on whether a donor egg is needed, how many embryo transfer attempts are included in the package, and whether full legal support is bundled in. According to specialist legal consultants, a standard programme runs at roughly €70,000, while a guaranteed-result programme with complete legal support comes to around €90,000. That's noticeably cheaper than the US, where comparable programmes often exceed $150,000, but more expensive than Ukraine, which remains one of the most affordable destinations in Europe.

§ 08

Why It's Worth Researching This in Advance Through MAPASGEN

For platform users, the Czech Republic often turns out to be one of the first options people look at when searching for a donor or surrogate, thanks to a workable combination of accessibility, well-developed medical infrastructure, and relatively moderate prices compared with Western Europe. But the exact combination of "medicine is well sorted, the law isn't" is precisely what makes this country one where doing your homework in advance matters more than almost anywhere else: it's worth researching the future child's citizenship situation ahead of time, discussing with a lawyer how parental rights will be recognized back in your own country (it won't necessarily mirror the Czech process automatically), and making sure your budget accounts not only for the medical protocol itself but for legal support — which, for surrogacy programmes, is a necessity rather than an optional extra.

§ 09

Points Worth Particular Attention

  • Lack of legal protection is the main risk of surrogacy in the Czech Republic. Before starting a programme, make sure to consult a lawyer who specialises specifically in reproductive law, not just a clinic coordinator.
  • Choose an agency or clinic with a transparent track record and genuine experience running international programmes — this reduces the risk of misunderstandings at various stages.
  • Clarify in advance how citizenship and travel documents for the newborn will be arranged, so you can bring the child home without delays.
  • Keep in mind that the final cost of a programme is often higher than the initial estimate, once you factor in flights, accommodation, notary services, and possibly multiple embryo transfer attempts.
  • Keep an eye on legislative developments: the 2024–2025 draft bills could meaningfully change the legal status of surrogacy in the country in the near future.
§ 10

Key Takeaways

  • Egg donation in the Czech Republic is legal, anonymous, and well regulated — one of the more reliable options in Europe in terms of medical oversight.
  • Surrogacy in the Czech Republic exists in a legal grey zone: it isn't banned, but it also isn't established by a dedicated law, and an agreement with a surrogate carries no binding legal force.
  • The default legal mother is the woman who gives birth; the genetic parents' rights are established through paternity acknowledgment followed by adoption.
  • Only the altruistic model is permitted — reimbursing expenses, not paying the surrogate for her services.
  • The whole process typically takes 16 to 18 months and costs on average between €70,000 and €90,000 — cheaper than US programmes but pricier than Ukrainian ones.
  • A consultation with a lawyer specialising in Czech reproductive law is essential before starting a programme.

Two centuries ago, a Czech scientist spotted a tiny nucleus inside an ordinary bird's egg — and in doing so, opened a door into the science of how life begins. Today, in that same country, that science has turned into a well-run medical practice: clinics know how to find donors, synchronise cycles, and carry out embryo transfers at a genuinely high professional standard. The law, though, is still catching up with the medicine — particularly when it comes to surrogacy, which exists more in practice than on the books. Anyone considering the Czech Republic for their own programme should pay equally close attention to both sides of this picture: the medical side, where things genuinely run well, and the legal side, where decisions need to be prepared for well in advance, with proper professional support.

Glossary

Surrogate
A woman who carries a pregnancy and gives birth to a child for another person or couple, becoming the child's biological mother, although legal parental rights transfer to the intended parents after birth or through legal proceedings.
Legal grey zone
An area of law or regulation that is not clearly defined, remains ambiguous or contradictory, creating uncertainty about the legality of a specific practice or activity.
Parental rights (в контексте Чехии)
A set of legal rights and responsibilities that parents or guardians have regarding a minor child, including the right to make decisions about their upbringing, education, health, and legal representation.
Agency
A private or charitable organization that mediates between intended parents and surrogates or donors, assisting with recruitment, matching, support, and management of administrative aspects of surrogacy or donation.
