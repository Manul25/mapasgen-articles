Tracing Finnish Roots in Russia: The Ingrian Finns of the St. Petersburg Land

§ 01

On February 27, 1617, in the village of Stolbovo, not far from Tikhvin, Russia and Sweden signed a peace treaty that ended the Time of Troubles on the northwestern front. Russia ceded to Sweden a vast region along the banks of the Neva and the Gulf of Finland — Ingria. The land, depopulated by wars and epidemics, was settled by the Swedish crown with Lutheran Finns from Karelia and Finland proper, and it was these 17th-century settlers who became the ancestors of the ethnic group later known as the Ingrian Finns. Just over a century later, in 1721, Peter the Great would return Ingria to Russia under the Treaty of Nystad — but the Finnish population would remain in place, right where St. Petersburg had by then already risen.

Two more centuries later, in 1929–1937, the Soviet government would begin methodically purging the territory of its "socially dangerous" Finnish element, and in 1943–1944 Germany and Finland would organize the evacuation of the remaining Ingrians to Finland — from where, under the 1944–1945 agreement, Soviet authorities would demand their forced return, not to their homeland near Leningrad, but to distant regions of the country. This fracture in the fate of an entire people is the key to understanding why the archival trail of the Ingrian Finns is so complicated, and at the same time so well documented, across several countries at once.

§ 02

Ingrian Finns and the indigenous Finno-Ugric peoples: who not to confuse

The first fork in the search is distinguishing the Ingrian Finns from the far older indigenous population of the same lands: the Izhorians and the Votes, Finno-Ugric peoples who lived along the banks of the Neva and the Gulf of Finland long before any colonization, Swedish or Russian. The Izhorians and Votes were not settlers but the original inhabitants; their languages (Izhorian and Votic) differ from Finnish, and their archival trace generally fades into far earlier Novgorod census books rather than the Swedish and Russian documents of the early modern era.

The Ingrian Finns, in the strict sense, are descendants of Lutheran settlers from Finland and Karelia who moved into the lands of Ingria, depopulated by the Time of Troubles and epidemics, during the period of Swedish rule from 1611 to 1721. It was they, not the Izhorians or Votes, who made up the bulk of the rural population around the future St. Petersburg by the 18th and 19th centuries, and who left behind the body of Lutheran church documents today known as the Ingrian Finnish archive.

§ 03

The Swedish period: colonizing depopulated land

The lands along the Neva had been all but emptied during the Time of Troubles: war, famine, and epidemics decimated both the Russian and the indigenous Finno-Ugric population. The Swedish administration, which acquired the region under the Treaty of Stolbovo, set out to deliberately settle it with Lutherans — first from neighboring Karelia, then from the interior regions of Finland; the resettlement was encouraged with tax incentives and closely tied to the Swedish crown's interest in securing a Protestant, rather than Orthodox or Catholic, population on its new frontier.

By the time Peter the Great reconquered Ingria during the Great Northern War and founded St. Petersburg at the mouth of the Neva in 1703, the Finnish Lutheran population already made up a significant share of the region's rural residents. The Russian administration did not expel or forcibly convert this flock — Finnish villages continued to exist literally on the outskirts of the new imperial capital, supplying milk, vegetables, and labor to the growing city right up until the Soviet era.

§ 04

The Lutheran Church of the St. Petersburg land: the parish system and its archives

Like the German colonists of the Volga, the Ingrian Finns were Lutherans and answered not to Orthodox consistories but to the St. Petersburg Evangelical Lutheran Consistory — the same church body that administered the Volga German parishes. The Finnish parishes of Ingria, however, formed a separate, geographically compact deanery within the consistory, with services held in Finnish and parish registers that in some congregations were kept in parallel in both Finnish and German (the administrative language).

The largest historical parishes — Toksovo, Koltushi, Lembolovo, Kylä-Saari (Murino), Rozhdestveno (Hietamäki), and dozens of others around St. Petersburg, Gatchina, and Yamburg (Kingisepp) — have their parish registers held today at the Central State Historical Archive of St. Petersburg (TsGIA SPb), where this category of documents is set apart in a distinct collection of Lutheran consistory records, considerably simplifying the search compared to Orthodox parish registers scattered across provincial archives.

§ 05

1929–1937: clearing the border zone

Ingria's proximity to the border with Finland, independent since 1917, made local Finns the first ethnic group to fall under Soviet suspicion of aiding a potential adversary. Already in 1929–1931, as part of dekulakization, a significant share of prosperous Finnish farming households was liquidated and their owners exiled to the northern regions and Siberia. In 1935–1936 followed a larger-scale operation — the forced resettlement of the Finnish population from the border zone of Leningrad Oblast into the interior of the country, officially justified on grounds of state security.

The culmination was the NKVD's Finnish operation of 1937–1938 — one of the "national operations" of the Great Terror, similar in logic to the Greek and Polish ones: ethnic Finnish identity itself became grounds for arrest on charges of espionage. The combined effect of the three waves of repression between 1929 and 1938 radically altered Ingria's demographics even before the start of World War II, stripping many Finnish families of both their land, the heads of their households, and, often, any archival trace at their prewar place of residence.

§ 06

1943–1944: evacuation to Finland and forced return

During the German occupation of the southern and central Ingrian districts in 1941–1943, the German command, by agreement with the government of Finland, organized the evacuation of the remaining Finnish population — around 63,000 people — to Finnish territory, officially on humanitarian grounds, but in fact both to use them as labor and as part of Finland's policy of consolidating related Finno-Ugric peoples.

After Finland withdrew from the war in 1944, the Soviet-Finnish armistice agreement demanded the repatriation of all Soviet citizens on Finnish territory, including the Ingrian Finns. But returning to the USSR in 1944–1945 did not mean returning home: entry into Leningrad Oblast was forbidden to Ingrian Finns until 1956, and repatriates were forcibly resettled in distant regions — Pskov, Novgorod, Yaroslavl, and others, as well as in Karelia. For genealogical research, this creates a distinctive break: the last prewar mention of a family in a parish near Leningrad, then the Finnish period of 1943–1944, documented in Finnish archives, and then postwar Soviet documents in an entirely different region of the country.

§ 07

Practical archives and sources

Prewar parish registers of the Finnish Lutheran congregations of Ingria are held primarily at the Central State Historical Archive of St. Petersburg; some materials also turn up at the Leningrad Regional State Archive in Vyborg and Lomonosov, where documents on territories once part of Leningrad Oblast are concentrated.

The Finnish period of 1943–1944 is documented at the National Archives of Finland (Kansallisarkisto) in Helsinki, where materials from the evacuation commissions were gathered, including family cards of evacuated Ingrians noting their prewar place of residence — for many families, this is the only source directly linking prewar and postwar geography. The Genealogical Society of Finland (Suomen Sukututkimusseura) and specialized Ingrian historical-cultural associations (Inkeri-liitto and others) maintain their own databases and indexes of Finnish parish registers, which are often more convenient for research than working directly with the original archival collections.

To reconstruct the postwar fate of repatriates, it's worth turning to the regional archives of the areas where they were forcibly resettled — Pskov, Novgorod, Yaroslavl, as well as the archives of Karelia; there is no single federal collection of "Ingrian repatriates" — the documents are scattered according to where each particular family was actually resettled.

§ 08

Where to start: a step-by-step plan

The first step is to confirm that the family in question is Ingrian Finnish rather than indigenous Izhorian or Votic: Lutheran faith and the Finnish (not Izhorian or Votic) language of family lore is a reliable marker. The second step is to identify the prewar parish based on residence geography (Toksovo, Koltushi, Lembolovo, and other names from family memory) and contact TsGIA SPb for the registers of that specific parish.

The third step — if evacuation to Finland in 1943–1944 is suspected — is to request materials from the National Archives of Finland through Suomen Sukututkimusseura, which maintains indexes of evacuation cards in Finnish. The fourth step, for reconstructing postwar history, is to determine the region of forced resettlement from oral family lore or 1950s Soviet documents (passports, certificates of release from special-settler status) and contact the corresponding regional archive directly, rather than expecting a single centralized collection.

§ 09

Conclusion

The history of the Ingrian Finns is a rare case where the genealogical trail of a single people crosses a state border three times over the course of two centuries: from Swedish colonization, through Russian imperial rule, to Soviet repression, Finnish evacuation, and forced postwar return to entirely different regions of the USSR. Each of these periods left documents in its own archival system — Russian imperial, Soviet regional, and Finnish national — and without understanding this sequence, a search easily gets stuck at the very first break.

The practical takeaway for a researcher: first confirm the family's Lutheran faith and Finnish (not Izhorian or Votic) identity, then find the prewar parish through TsGIA SPb, and if there are traces of the 1943–1944 evacuation, be sure to consult Finnish archives through Suomen Sukututkimusseura — and only then, to reconstruct the family's fate after 1944, search the regional archive of the place of actual forced resettlement, which almost never coincides with the family's prewar homeland near St. Petersburg.

§ 010

Glossary

Ingria — a historic region along the banks of the Neva and the Gulf of Finland, including the territory of present-day St. Petersburg and its surroundings.

Ingrian Finns — descendants of Lutheran settlers from Finland and Karelia who populated Ingria during the period of Swedish rule from 1611 to 1721.

Izhorians and Votes — indigenous Finno-Ugric peoples of Ingria who lived on these lands before Swedish colonization; distinct from the Ingrian Finns in both origin and language.

The NKVD Finnish operation — the 1937–1938 repressive campaign against Soviet Finns on charges of spying for Finland.

Kansallisarkisto — the National Archives of Finland in Helsinki, holding materials from the 1943–1944 evacuation commissions.

Suomen Sukututkimusseura — the Genealogical Society of Finland, which maintains indexes of parish registers and evacuation documents of the Ingrian Finns.
