Tracing Korean Roots in Russia: The History of the Koryo-saram

§ 01

In the summer of 1863, several Korean peasant families, fleeing famine in Korea's northern provinces, secretly crossed the Tumen River and settled in the Tizinhe valley in the south of Russian Primorye — then an almost uninhabited fringe of the empire, annexed just five years earlier. This was the beginning of the history of the Koryo-saram, ethnic Koreans of Russia and the former Soviet space. Exactly seventy-four years later, in September 1937, over the course of eleven days, the NKVD would load some 172,000 descendants of those first settlers onto freight trains and haul them off to Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan — the first total ethnic deportation in Soviet history, without a single exception and without trial, one that would become a grim precedent for all subsequent operations of its kind.

Between these two dates lies the history of a community that managed to become one of the centers of the Korean national liberation movement against Japanese colonization, to survive one of the fastest and most complete deportations of the 20th century, and ultimately to scatter across the entire former Soviet space — from millionaire collective farms in Kazakhstan to present-day diasporas in Rostov and Krasnodar. For anyone searching for Korean roots in Russia, understanding this chronology — geographic as much as temporal — is the main tool for navigating the archives of three modern states.

§ 02

From famine in Korea to Primorye: the resettlement of the 1860s–1910s

The first wave of Korean migration to Russia was triggered by catastrophic famine in the northern Korean provinces of Hamgyong in the 1860s — even though Korea itself remained at the time a closed state that strictly forbade emigration on pain of death. Nevertheless, by 1869–1870, after another crop failure, thousands of Korean families illegally crossed the border and settled in southern Primorye, which Russia had received from China under the 1860 Convention of Peking and which badly needed labor to develop a nearly empty region.

By the early 20th century, and especially after Japan's annexation of Korea in 1910, the migration flow was joined not only by peasant refugees but also by political émigrés — members of the Korean resistance, for whom Russian Primorye became one of the main bases of the anti-Japanese struggle. The Korean quarter of Sinhanchon ("New Korean Settlement"), which arose near Vladivostok, turned into the de facto capital of the Korean diaspora and a center of the national liberation movement, home to Korean newspapers, schools, and underground organizations resisting Japan's colonial regime. By 1917, around 100,000 ethnic Koreans lived in Primorye.

§ 03

The Soviet Far East: between support and suspicion

In the early Soviet decades, the Korean population of the Far East received a certain degree of cultural autonomy: Korean schools, publishing houses, national village councils, and collective farms operated, and instruction was conducted in Korean. At the same time, Soviet authorities — much as they would later do with the Ingrian Finns and the Polish population of border districts — increasingly viewed Koreans as a potentially unreliable group precisely because of the region's border location, next to Japan, which by then had occupied not only Korea but also Manchuria, turning the region into a zone of acute military-political confrontation.

Tellingly, the 1937 deportation of Koreans is hard to call spontaneous: in many ways, it was a pilot project of Soviet ethnic policy. A resolution of the Council of People's Commissars and the Central Committee of the All-Union Communist Party of August 21, 1937, justified the expulsion directly on the grounds of "preventing the penetration of Japanese espionage," and the operation itself later served as an organizational and ideological model for the deportations of Chechens, Crimean Tatars, Volga Germans, and other peoples in the following years of the war.

§ 04

September 1937: total deportation without exceptions

The deportation, carried out between September and October 1937, differed from most subsequent NKVD ethnic operations in one crucial respect: it was absolutely total, with no division into "unreliable" and "loyal" — the entire Korean population of the Far Eastern Krai was subject to expulsion without exception, including party members, Red Army soldiers, and Soviet officials of Korean nationality. Around 172,000 people were loaded onto freight trains and, over the course of roughly a month, transported thousands of kilometers to the Kazakh and Uzbek SSRs, often with no clear idea of their final destination and no chance to bring their belongings.

Conditions during transport and the first winter in the new location proved extremely harsh: deportees were often unloaded onto the open steppe with no housing, and mortality in the first months was significant, though Soviet statistics never preserved exact overall figures. For genealogical research, this creates a distinctive break in the documents: the last mention of a family at its Far Eastern place of residence in 1937, and the first — already in the records of the special settlements and collective farms of Kazakhstan or Uzbekistan, with no smooth archival transition between the two.

§ 05

Life in Central Asia: collective farms, the Koryo-mar language, and a new identity

Despite its tragic beginning, over the following postwar decades the deported Koreans of Central Asia managed to build some of the most economically successful collective farms in Soviet Central Asia, specializing in rice and vegetable cultivation; a number of farms, including the renowned "Polyarnaya Zvezda" kolkhoz (formerly "Politotdel") under the leadership of Kim Pyong Hwa, achieved millionaire status and saw their chairmen awarded the title Hero of Socialist Labor — a rare case of a deported people achieving this kind of recognition within the Soviet system so quickly.

It was precisely in Central Asia that the distinct identity of the Koryo-saram took shape, as a separate ethnic community distinct both from mainland Koreans and from the pre-Soviet Koreans of the Russian Far East. The key marker of this identity is the Koryo-mar language, a dialect of Korean based on the northern Hamgyong varieties, considerably different from modern literary Korean (both the southern and northern standards) and virtually unintelligible to speakers of the standard language. For genealogical research, the preservation or, conversely, the loss of Koryo-mar within a particular family can serve as an indirect indicator of the depth and character of assimilation.

§ 06

Post-Soviet dispersion: return migration and new diasporas

After the collapse of the USSR, the Koryo-saram found themselves divided by the state borders of the newly independent Central Asian republics, and a significant part of the community began a new migration — this time to the southern regions of Russia (Rostov Oblast, Krasnodar Krai, Stavropol Krai), where new compact Korean farming communities took shape, as well as to the Republic of Korea, which since the 1990s has run streamlined visa and labor programs for ethnic Koreans abroad (compatriots, or dongpo).

This third migration in the sequence — from Central Asia to Russia and Korea — adds yet another layer of archival research for today's descendants of deported families: documents on relocation, registration, and acquisition of citizenship after 1991, which are usually held not in historical archives but in the current migration and consular archives of the respective countries.

§ 08

Where to start: a step-by-step plan

The first step is to establish the family's Far Eastern (prewar or early Soviet) period through RGIA DV in Vladivostok: family lists and census materials can trace the family's history back to the first settlers of the late 19th century. The second step is to find documents of the 1937 deportation and the subsequent special-settlement registration in the regional archives of Kazakhstan or Uzbekistan, guided by oral family lore about the place of actual resettlement.

The third step is to request the rehabilitation case file through the regional FSB office for the family's prewar Far Eastern place of residence: such files often contain a brief but precise biographical note that fills in the missing links between the Far Eastern and Central Asian periods. The fourth step, especially productive in the absence of archival documents, is to contact the All-Russian Association of Koreans or regional Korean cultural centers, which hold informal but extensive collections of oral testimony about the deportation and postwar resettlement.

§ 09

Conclusion

The history of the Koryo-saram is unusual in that it fits within the lifespan of just three or four generations, yet crosses the borders of four states: flight from famine out of a closed imperial Korea, half a century of life in the Russian Far East as an alternately encouraged and suspected minority, the total deportation of 1937 to Soviet Central Asia, and finally a new dispersion after 1991 — into Russia and the Republic of Korea. Each of these periods left documents in its own archival system, and it is precisely the total, exceptionless nature of the 1937 deportation that makes the break between the Far Eastern and Central Asian periods especially sharp, requiring a targeted rather than a general archival search.

The practical takeaway for a researcher: start with RGIA DV in Vladivostok to reconstruct the Far Eastern period, then go directly to the archives of Kazakhstan or Uzbekistan for the place of actual resettlement after the deportation, always check rehabilitation case files through the regional FSB, and at every stage draw on the resources of Korean cultural organizations, which often fill the gaps in official archives with oral history gathered from the families who lived through the deportation and their descendants.

§ 010

Glossary

Koryo-saram — the self-designation of ethnic Koreans in Russia and the post-Soviet states, descendants of settlers from Korea beginning in the 1860s.

Sinhanchon — a Korean quarter near Vladivostok, the largest center of the Korean diaspora and the national liberation movement in the early 20th century.

Koryo-mar — a dialect of Korean based on the northern Hamgyong varieties, which developed among the Koryo-saram; considerably different from modern literary Korean.

Special settlers — the status of deported peoples in the USSR, entailing restricted freedom of movement and mandatory registration at the place of forced settlement.

RGIA DV — the Russian State Historical Archive of the Far East in Vladivostok, the main source of documents on the Korean population of Primorye in the prewar and early Soviet periods.

Dongpo — a Korean term for ethnic Koreans abroad ("compatriots"), covered by the Republic of Korea's streamlined visa and labor programs.
