Reciprocal IVF (ROPA): How Two Bodies Share Motherhood

In July 1978, Louise Brown was born in England — the first person in history conceived through in vitro fertilization. Back then, nobody involved in that story could have guessed what unexpected purpose IVF would be put to almost three decades later. In 2005, Spain became the third country in the world — after the Netherlands and Belgium — to grant same-sex marriage full legal recognition. The very next year, in 2006, the Spanish parliament passed a law on assisted reproductive technologies that, almost as a side effect, gave rise to a method nobody had set out to invent: ROPA.

The acronym ROPA stands for Reception of Oocytes from Partner. The idea behind the method is simple and, at the same time, quietly revolutionary: eggs are taken from one woman in a couple, fertilized with donor sperm, and the resulting embryo is carried by the other woman. The child ends up genetically linked to one mother and physically linked — through pregnancy, birth, and all the hormonal and immune exchange that comes with carrying a child — to the other. For lesbian couples, it's the only path to parenthood where both partners are biologically involved in bringing the same child into the world.

How the Name Came About, and Why Spain

Spain's 2006 law didn't formally mention ROPA at all — it simply established equal access to assisted reproductive technologies for all women, regardless of marital status or sexual orientation. But it was precisely the broad interpretation of that law that let clinics offer lesbian couples something new: splitting genetic and gestational motherhood between two living partners, rather than between a partner and an anonymous donor. Since 2007, Spain has formally recognized dual motherhood — both women in a couple are listed as parents on the child's birth certificate, with no additional court proceedings required.

In 2023, Spanish law took another step forward: Law 4/2023, on guarantees for the rights of the LGBTQ+ community, scrapped the judicial-adoption requirement for the non-carrying partner even in unregistered couples — her consent, recorded at the civil registry at the time of birth, is now enough. That removed one of the last bureaucratic hurdles that, for years, had forced couples through an extra legal process after the child was already born.

The Medical Protocol: What Actually Happens

The ROPA protocol is built around two parallel medical journeys, staggered in time. The partner acting as egg donor goes through standard ovarian stimulation — the same 10 to 14 days of hormone injections and monitoring scans, during which the doctor tracks the growth of dominant follicles, just as in any ordinary IVF cycle. Once the follicles reach the right size, retrieval is carried out under light anesthesia. The eggs collected are then fertilized with sperm from the chosen donor, usually through ICSI — injecting a single sperm cell directly into the egg under a microscope.

Meanwhile, the other partner prepares her endometrium: a course of estrogen thickens the uterine lining to a state where an embryo can successfully implant. The embryos are cultured in the lab for three to five days, often in time-lapse incubators that let embryologists track the pace of cell division and select the embryo with the strongest potential without repeatedly removing it from a controlled environment. Transferring the chosen embryo into the prepared uterus is the final — and shortest — step of the entire protocol.

Who Tends to Donate, and Who Tends to Carry

A large Spanish study pooling data from eighteen clinics between 2011 and 2020 offers a rare look at the method's real-world statistics, rather than just a description of the procedure. It turned out that the partner acting as egg donor is, on average, three and a half years younger than the one carrying the pregnancy — couples, whether by intuition or on a doctor's advice, tend to assign roles based on age and ovarian reserve.

One striking detail from that study: for 13% of couples, the medical work-up before ROPA was the first time a fertility issue ever came to light — diminished ovarian reserve, endometriosis, or polycystic ovary syndrome. Counting couples over 38 as well, more than half had some such finding. In other words, for a substantial share of lesbian couples, ROPA isn't just a way to symbolically share motherhood — it's also a practical solution to a medical issue that might otherwise have gone unnoticed.

The Legal Side: Why It Varies So Much by Country

While the medical protocol for ROPA is nearly identical wherever it's permitted, its legal status across Europe looks like a genuine patchwork. In some countries, both parents are automatically listed on the child's documents; in others, the law simply doesn't recognize a lesbian couple as eligible for medical assistance at all; and in a few places, a formal ban exists on paper while clinics, in practice, find workarounds.

Germany and France show that a ban can come from entirely different roots. In Germany, egg donation is banned outright — the 1990 Embryo Protection Act makes ROPA impossible regardless of a couple's orientation. In France, by contrast, lesbian couples have had access to IVF and donor insemination since 2021, but transferring an egg between partners is treated as a form of donation that must remain anonymous — so, somewhat paradoxically, it's legally off the table between two women who love each other.

The Emotional Side: What Both Partners Go Through

One of the fears specialists hear most often from patients preparing to be the carrying partner: if I didn't provide the egg, am I somehow reduced to a vessel for someone else's genetic material? Modern science answers that question with a firm no — and not out of politeness, but on the strength of epigenetics. While the carrying partner doesn't pass on nuclear DNA, the uterine environment quite literally shapes how that DNA gets expressed — which genes switch on and which stay quiet — through hormones, nutrition, blood supply, and dozens of other factors in the intrauterine environment. That's not a metaphorical bond; it's a measurable biological one, just of a different kind than genetic kinship.

The partner providing the eggs goes through her own range of emotions too — and they're not always straightforwardly positive. Some find it hard to accept that they won't be the one physically pregnant, won't feel the first kicks, won't go through labour. Good clinics build psychological counselling into the protocol for real reasons, not as a formality: talking through these feelings together before treatment starts lowers the risk that one partner ends up facing her own anxiety alone in the middle of an already-underway medical process.

What to Do About Frozen Embryos

If the retrieval yields several good-quality eggs, fertilization and culturing typically produce more than one embryo — and usually only the strongest one gets transferred. The rest are cryopreserved for future attempts. That raises a question worth settling in writing before the protocol even begins, rather than leaving for later: what happens to the frozen embryos if the couple separates, if one partner dies, or if they decide not to have a second child. In most jurisdictions where ROPA is legal, both partners hold equal rights to those embryos regardless of whose egg created them — which is exactly why a written agreement, drawn up calmly ahead of time, protects both sides far better than a verbal understanding ever could.

Reciprocal ROPA and Other Paths to Parenthood

In a handful of countries, including Austria, a less common variant is also practised — reciprocal, or "double," ROPA: each partner simultaneously acts as an egg donor for the other and as a recipient of the other's embryo. If both transfers succeed, both women become pregnant at roughly the same time, and both children end up biologically connected to both mothers, in a mirrored arrangement. It's a complicated, expensive option that isn't always medically advisable, but it shows just how inventively couples and clinics have approached the idea of shared motherhood.

ROPA is far from the only route open to lesbian couples. Standard IVF with donor sperm, where only one partner becomes pregnant and is genetically linked to the child, is cheaper and medically simpler. Intrauterine insemination works well if the fertility of the partner planning to carry isn't a concern. Co-parenting with a known sperm donor who stays involved in the child's life is a separate path with its own legal and emotional considerations. The choice between these options comes down not just to medical indications, but to how important it is for a particular couple that both partners be biologically involved.

Key Takeaways

  • ROPA is an IVF method where eggs are taken from one partner while the other carries the pregnancy; the child is biologically connected to both.
  • The method took shape in Spain alongside the 2005–2006 laws on marriage equality and assisted reproduction; Spain has recognized dual motherhood since 2007, and without judicial adoption since 2023.
  • According to a large study, the egg donor averages 3.5 years younger than the recipient, and pre-treatment screening reveals a previously unknown fertility issue in 13% of couples.
  • ROPA's legal status varies sharply by country: legal in Spain, Portugal, the UK, Denmark, and Austria; banned in Germany and France; effectively unavailable in Greece, the Czech Republic, and Ukraine.
  • Even without passing on DNA, the carrying parent shapes the child's development through epigenetic mechanisms tied to the intrauterine environment.
  • It's worth settling the fate of frozen embryos in writing before the protocol begins, not afterward.

Glossary

  • ROPA (Reception of Oocytes from Partner) — an IVF method in which one partner's eggs are fertilized with donor sperm while the other partner carries the resulting embryo.
  • Genetic mother — the partner whose egg was used to conceive the child.
  • Gestational mother — the partner who carries the pregnancy and gives birth.
  • Ovarian reserve — the stock of eggs currently available to a woman's ovaries; it declines with age.
  • Ovarian stimulation — a course of hormone medication that prompts several eggs to mature in a single cycle instead of just one.
  • Follicle retrieval — a short procedure, done under anesthesia, to collect mature eggs from the ovaries.
  • ICSI — a fertilization technique in which a single sperm cell is injected directly into an egg under a microscope.
  • Endometrium — the uterine lining an embryo attaches to; it's prepared with estrogen ahead of transfer.
  • Embryo cryopreservation — freezing unused embryos in liquid nitrogen for future transfer attempts.
  • Epigenetics — mechanisms that affect which genes switch on and function without changing the DNA sequence itself; the uterine environment during pregnancy can influence these mechanisms.
  • Reciprocal (double) ROPA — a protocol variant in which each partner simultaneously acts as an egg donor and as a recipient of the other's embryo.

Less than half a century separates the first test-tube baby in 1978 from a method that splits motherhood between two women — and for most of that time, the idea seemed unthinkable not because of the technology, but because of the law. Today, ROPA remains a field where medicine has outpaced legislation, though unevenly from country to country: in one place it's a routine procedure with a clear legal outcome, in another it's formally off the table. Anyone considering this path should look closely at both sides of it — the medical side, and exactly what the law says in the specific place where treatment will happen.
