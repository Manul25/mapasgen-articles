The White Emigration: Where to Find the Archives of Russia Abroad

§ 01

In 1922, the Norwegian polar explorer and diplomat Fridtjof Nansen proposed a solution to the League of Nations for a problem no single state could solve on its own: hundreds of thousands of former subjects of the Russian Empire had fled the revolution and Civil War, held no Soviet passport, refused to recognise Soviet authority, and yet had no valid identity document of any kind. The result was the 'Nansen passport' — an identity certificate for stateless refugees, recognised by more than 50 countries and used by tens of thousands of Russian émigrés until the 1930s. Today, a mention of a Nansen passport in a family archive or an old letter is almost a guaranteed sign that you're dealing with a first-wave refugee rather than a later Soviet or post-Soviet émigré — and that fact immediately points to which archives to search next.

§ 02

How Many There Were, and Where They Went

Historians' estimates vary, but somewhere between one and a half and two million people left Russia after the Civil War — soldiers of the defeated White armies, the nobility, the clergy, the intelligentsia, Cossacks, and ordinary civilians who simply refused to accept the new regime. The main centres of resettlement formed around a handful of cities, and an émigré's archival trail is almost always tied to one of them. Constantinople was the first stop for those evacuated through Crimea in 1920, including the famous exodus of General Wrangel's army. Berlin in the early 1920s was so saturated with Russian émigrés that the Charlottenburg district was jokingly nicknamed 'Charlottengrad'. Prague, thanks to the Czechoslovak government's 'Russian Action' programme of scholarships and support for Russian students and scholars, became a major intellectual centre. Belgrade and the Kingdom of Yugoslavia took in a substantial share of military personnel and clergy. Harbin, in Manchuria, grew into a fully-fledged Russian city on Chinese territory, populated by employees of the Chinese Eastern Railway and their descendants. But the largest and most enduring centre remained Paris, particularly its suburb of Billancourt, home to the Renault factory, where a great many former officers found work as factory hands.

§ 03

The Bakhmeteff Archive: Émigré Memory, Gathered in New York

One of the largest archives on the history of Russian emigration sits not in Europe but in New York, at Columbia University. The archive is named after Boris Bakhmeteff, the last ambassador of Russia's Provisional Government to the United States, who remained in emigration after the revolution and, in 1951, became one of the founders of this collection. For decades the Bakhmeteff Archive systematically gathered personal documents, diaries, correspondence and photographs of first-wave émigrés, and today it holds hundreds of separate personal and family collections — for descendants of émigrés, this is one of the most likely addresses where letters, memoirs or photographs of a specific ancestor might turn up, provided the family kept any connection to the United States at all.

§ 04

The Hoover Institution: A California Collection on the Russian Catastrophe

The archive of the Hoover Institution on War, Revolution and Peace at Stanford University was originally created to document the First World War, but quickly expanded to cover the Russian Revolution and the emigration that followed — the institution's founder, future US President Herbert Hoover, personally organised humanitarian aid to famine-stricken Russia in the early 1920s, and through those channels gained access to an enormous body of documents. The Hoover collection includes archives of the White governments, the personal papers of military and political figures of the emigration, and one of the world's largest collections of émigré periodicals — newspapers and magazines published by the Russian diaspora around the world, which frequently carried obituaries, notices seeking lost relatives, and lists of graduates from émigré educational institutions.

§ 05

The House of Russian Emigration: Memory Returned to Moscow

The Alexander Solzhenitsyn House of Russian Emigration in Moscow is a comparatively young institution, opened in 1995, but it serves a unique function: it gathers archives and personal collections from émigrés and their descendants, who, after the collapse of the USSR, began handing over family documents to their ancestors' homeland. For a researcher, this means that material on the same émigré family may be physically split between Western archives, where the documents ended up at the time of emigration, and the Moscow archive, to which post-Soviet heirs later donated them — and for a complete picture, it's worth checking both directions.

§ 06

The Police Prefecture and the Foreigner's File: An Unexpected Source in France

For émigrés who settled in France, one of the most underrated sources isn't an archive of the Russian community at all, but French state bureaucracy. Every foreigner residing in France during the interwar period was required to register with the police prefecture of their département, and a personal file — a dossier d'étranger — was opened on them, which could include a photograph, fingerprints, addresses of residence across the years, and details of any change in status. These files are held today in the départemental archives (Archives départementales) for the place where the émigré lived, not centrally in Paris, so a search requires knowing exactly which département the person resided in. If the émigré later obtained French citizenship, the naturalisation file (dossier de naturalisation) is held separately, at the French National Archives in Pithiviers, and usually contains a far more detailed biographical record than the foreigner's file — sometimes including witness statements vouching for the applicant's good character.

§ 07

The Church Abroad: Registers Kept Outside Moscow

After the revolution, part of the Russian Orthodox clergy abroad refused to recognise the leadership of the Moscow Patriarchate, which had fallen under Soviet state control, and formed a separate jurisdiction — the Russian Orthodox Church Outside Russia (ROCOR), which formally reunited with the Moscow Patriarchate only in 2007. For nearly the entire twentieth century, ROCOR parishes in Europe, the United States, Australia and South America kept their own registers of births, marriages and deaths among émigrés — entirely separate from the Soviet civil registration system and from any registers that might have existed in the ancestral homeland before the revolution. For descendants of émigrés, this is often the only source recording the exact date and place of an event that took place after emigration, and such records need to be sought directly in the archives of the specific ROCOR parish or the diocesan administration covering that region.

§ 08

Harbin: An Emigration That Had to Flee Twice

The story of the Harbin branch of the Russian emigration is more complicated than its European counterpart, because the Harbin Russians had to flee not once but twice. After 1920, Harbin and the railway zone of the Chinese Eastern Railway became home to tens of thousands of Russians — railway employees, military men, clergy — who built a fully functioning Russian city, complete with schools, churches and newspapers, on Chinese territory. But in 1945 Soviet troops occupied Manchuria, and by 1949 the communists had won in China, forcing the Harbin Russians to evacuate again — this time to Australia, Brazil, the United States and other countries that took in refugees via transit camps in the Philippines. For descendants of Harbin Russians, this means an ancestor's archival trail often splits into three layers: the Chinese period (registers of Harbin churches, Chinese Eastern Railway documents), the transitional camp period, and finally the country of eventual settlement after 1949 — and each layer may preserve different documents about the very same person.

§ 09

Sainte-Geneviève-des-Bois: A Cemetery as a Genealogical Source

The Russian cemetery at Sainte-Geneviève-des-Bois outside Paris, founded in 1927, became the resting place of several generations of émigrés from the first wave and those that followed — from participants in the White movement and figures of culture to their children and grandchildren — and today it holds one of the largest collections of Russian gravestones outside Russia itself. A gravestone inscription often holds more information than it first appears to: beyond a name and dates, many monuments list a military rank, the name of a regiment or educational institution, a wife's maiden name, and sometimes even the name of a home town or province in Russia. A full list of burials and photographs of headstones have been compiled in several open databases maintained by associations of émigré descendants, and for a family that's lost track of a specific ancestor in France, the cemetery's records sometimes prove faster than official French agencies. A similar function for émigrés worldwide is served by the multi-volume project 'Forgotten Graves No More' (Nezabytye Mogily), which Moscow's House of Russian Emigration has maintained since the 1990s: a systematic compilation of obituaries and death notices for prominent figures of the emigration, gathered from émigré periodicals across many countries and decades. While the project focuses mainly on notable figures rather than ordinary émigrés, for families whose ancestor held any kind of public, military or cultural position within the diaspora, it's worth checking this compilation separately from archival requests.

§ 11

Five Mistakes in Researching Émigré Ancestors

  • Searching for documents about an émigré exclusively in Russian archives, without checking the archives of the country where they actually settled.
  • Overlooking the fact that in France a foreigner's file is held at the place of residence within a specific département, not centrally in Paris.
  • Ignoring émigré periodicals — old newspapers and magazines of the Russian diaspora frequently published obituaries and notices seeking lost relatives.
  • Assuming an émigré's birth or marriage register must be in Soviet or Russian fonds, when the event actually took place abroad and was recorded by a ROCOR parish.
  • Failing to check whether descendants donated the family archive to the House of Russian Emigration — part of the material today sits physically in Moscow rather than in the West.
§ 12

Search Checklist

  1. Establish which centre of emigration (Constantinople, Berlin, Prague, Belgrade, Harbin, Paris) your ancestor settled in, and in what year.
  2. Check the Bakhmeteff and Hoover Institution archives for a personal collection under that surname.
  3. If your ancestor lived in France, request the dossier d'étranger from the relevant départemental archive and, where applicable, the dossier de naturalisation at Pithiviers.
  4. Check whether your ancestor's birth, marriage or death was recorded by a ROCOR parish in the region where they lived.
  5. Search for the surname in catalogues of émigré periodicals — a mention in an obituary or notice often supplies an exact date and additional relatives' names.

The archival trail of the White emigration is scattered across the world in much the same way the émigrés themselves were: some documents ended up in American university collections, some in the archives of European départements and cities, some in the fonds of a church jurisdiction that existed entirely outside the Soviet system, and some, in recent decades, have made their way back to Moscow along with descendants. A systematic understanding of this geography turns the search from random wandering into a coherent route through a handful of specific archives.

§ 13

Glossary

Nansen passport — an identity certificate for stateless refugees, introduced at Fridtjof Nansen's initiative in 1922.

Bakhmeteff Archive — a collection of personal documents of the Russian emigration at Columbia University, named after the diplomat Boris Bakhmeteff.

Hoover Institution Archives — a collection of documents on the Russian Revolution and emigration at Stanford University.

The Alexander Solzhenitsyn House of Russian Emigration — a Moscow institution gathering archives of émigrés and their descendants since 1995.

Dossier d'étranger — a personal file opened by the French police prefecture on every foreign resident of the country.

ROCOR (the Russian Orthodox Church Outside Russia) — a jurisdiction of Russian Orthodoxy outside the USSR, existing separately from the Moscow Patriarchate until 2007.

Sainte-Geneviève-des-Bois — a Russian cemetery outside Paris, founded in 1927, the resting place of several generations of émigrés.

'Forgotten Graves No More' (Nezabytye Mogily) — a multi-volume necrology of prominent figures of the Russian emigration, maintained by the House of Russian Emigration.
