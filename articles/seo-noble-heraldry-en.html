Noble Roots: How to Search for Ancestors in Genealogical Registers and the General Armorial

§ 01

In 1797, Emperor Paul I established the General Armorial of the Noble Families of the Russian Empire — an official collection of coats of arms, into which a family could be entered only after documentary confirmation of noble status and a ruling by the Heraldry Office (Geroldiya), the special state body responsible for matters of the nobility. By 1917, twenty volumes of the Armorial had been compiled, covering several thousand families — and the very fact of a family's inclusion in the Armorial means that, at some point, a substantial package of documents proving its origin was gathered and verified, part of which survives in archives to this day. For a descendant of a noble family, the General Armorial is not just an attractive image with a heraldic description, but a pointer to a specific archival case, which can be the starting point for much deeper research.

§ 02

Six Parts of the Genealogical Register: Why Nobility Wasn't All the Same

The genealogical registers of the nobility, kept in every governorate from 1785 under Catherine II's Charter to the Nobility, divided nobles into six parts depending on the origin of their title. The first part listed nobles who received their rank by imperial grant; the second, those who earned nobility through military service; the third, those who earned it through civil service; the fourth, foreign noble families; the fifth, the titled nobility (princes, counts, barons); and the sixth, the most honoured, ancient noble families whose noble origin could be traced back more than a hundred years before the register was established. Knowing which part of the genealogical register a family was listed under, a researcher immediately understands what kind of documents to look for: for the sixth part, these would be sixteenth- and seventeenth-century documents; for the second or third, considerably later and far more clearly recorded service records.

§ 03

The Service Record: A Military or Civil Career as a Source of Biography

For nobles who earned their rank through military or civil service — the overwhelming majority by the nineteenth century — the principal source of a detailed biography is the service record (formulyarny spisok), kept throughout a career and updated with every promotion or transfer. A service record typically contained not just dates of service and ranks, but details of marital status, property holdings, participation in military campaigns and decorations — in effect, an official biography of the official or officer, compiled by contemporaries. Such documents are held today at the Russian State Historical Archive (for civil ranks) and the Russian State Military History Archive (for military ranks), as well as in the fonds of specific ministries and departments.

§ 04

The Heraldry Office and the Nobility Case File: Bureaucracy Working for Genealogy

To be entered in the genealogical register, a member of a family had to submit a petition to the governorate's Noble Deputy Assembly, accompanied by documents confirming noble origin — and the assembly, in turn, forwarded the materials to the Heraldry Office (later the Senate's Department of Heraldry) for final approval. As a result, every noble family that went through this procedure had an archival nobility case file opened on it, which could include copies of birth and baptismal registers, marriage certificates, documents on the acquisition of estates, and, particularly valuable, genealogical charts compiled by the petitioners themselves, referencing earlier documents and family tradition. These case files are held today in the fonds of the governorate Noble Deputy Assemblies, in regional archives for the place the family was registered.

§ 05

Personal Nobles and Odnodvortsy: Nobility Without a Title

The Russian estate system recognised several categories formally adjacent to the nobility but lacking the full set of privileges. Personal nobility, unlike hereditary nobility, was not passed down and was granted for reaching a certain rank or receiving certain decorations — the descendants of a personal noble reverted to their previous estate. The odnodvortsy were a separate and fairly numerous category, descended from the service people of the southern and south-eastern frontiers of the Muscovite state in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, formally equal in certain rights to the petty nobility, but in practice living a peasant way of life, with their own land allotments and their own system of record-keeping, closer to that of the peasantry than the nobility. For descendants of odnodvortsy, archival research is often built along lines similar to a search for state peasants rather than classical noble genealogy.

§ 06

Archives of the Nobility Abroad: Émigré Genealogical Societies

After 1917, a substantial portion of the Russian nobility found itself in emigration, and specialised organisations arose to preserve genealogical memory — the best known remains the Russian Nobility Association, re-established in emigration and subsequently returned to Russia, along with the archive of the Union of Russian Nobles in France and similar associations in the United States and the United Kingdom. From the twentieth century, these organisations maintained their own genealogical registers for émigré descendants of noble families, frequently accepting entries based on family tradition and documents not preserved in official Russian archives — for descendants of an émigré noble family, approaching such a society can sometimes yield results faster than a request to a state archive.

§ 07

The Titled Nobility: Princes, Counts and Barons

The fifth part of the genealogical register set apart the titled nobility — holders of princely, comital or baronial rank — and for this category there exists an additional layer of sources not relevant to untitled families. The letters patent for a title, issued personally by the emperor, recorded not just the bare fact of the grant but frequently a detailed justification — for precisely what merits the family was awarded the title, which is itself a valuable historical source. Some Russian princely families traced their origin not to a grant but to the Rurikids or Gediminids — that is, to the ancient ruling dynasties of Rus and Lithuania — and for such families there exist separate, specialised genealogical compendia tracing the line back a thousand years, considerably deeper than archival documents of the modern era allow. It's worth bearing in mind that a baronial title in the Russian Empire frequently belonged to Baltic German families who had received it even before the Baltic lands were annexed, in which case the archival trail of the title is worth seeking not only in Russian but also in German or Baltic sources.

§ 08

How to Read a Coat of Arms Description: The Language of Blazon

An entry in the General Armorial is accompanied not only by an image but by a textual heraldic description — a blazon, composed according to the strict rules of a specialised professional language, in which colours, charges and their placement on the shield are encoded using specific terms of French origin: azure (blue), gules (red), vert (green), argent and or in place of white and yellow. A blazon frequently contains encoded information about a family's origin or about the merits of the specific ancestor who earned the right to bear the arms — for instance, the depiction of a particular animal or weapon could point to a military feat that gave rise to the family's noble status. The ability to read a blazon isn't required to begin a search, but it substantially helps in interpreting a find once a family's coat of arms has already been located in the Armorial.

§ 09

Ancestral Estates and Manors: Another Archival Trail

Beyond documents on noble status itself, a significant source remains documentation on land and estate ownership — deeds of purchase and sale of estates, land survey plans, and inventories of property drawn up when an inheritance was divided among brothers and sisters. These documents are held in the fonds of governorate peasant affairs boards and in the fonds of notarial offices for the relevant region, and for manors that have survived to the present day as architectural monuments, separate local-history studies and museum collections frequently exist, which may hold information about owners not included in the main body of genealogical documents. An estate can thus become more than just a pleasant detail of family legend — it can be an independent entry point into archival research, especially where its present-day status (a museum, a heritage site) implies the existence of its own historical archive.

§ 11

Five Mistakes in Noble Genealogical Research

  • Searching for documents only in the General Armorial, without understanding it's a pointer rather than a family's full archive.
  • Failing to determine which of the six parts of the genealogical register a family was listed under, and so not knowing which era to search for the first documents in.
  • Confusing personal nobility with hereditary nobility and looking for inherited rights where none formally existed.
  • Applying classical noble research logic to odnodvortsy, whose documents are structured closer to those of peasants.
  • Failing to check émigré genealogical societies for families that left Russia after 1917.
§ 12

Search Checklist

  • Check the General Armorial for the surname and note the volume and coat-of-arms number, if an entry exists.
  • Establish which part of the governorate's genealogical register the family was listed under, to understand the approximate era of the first documents.
  • Request the nobility case file from the fonds of the relevant region's governorate Noble Deputy Assembly.
  • For an ancestor in military or civil service, request the service record from RGVIA or RGIA.
  • If the family emigrated after 1917, check the archives of the Russian Nobility Association and similar émigré organisations.

Noble genealogical research differs from most other kinds in that the system was originally created by bureaucracy, for bureaucracy: every noble rank had to be documented, and that need for proof left behind a surprisingly detailed archival trail. Knowing the structure of genealogical registers, service records and nobility case files turns the search from a hope for luck into systematic work with sources that, unlike many peasant archives, were created specifically to prove origin.

§ 13

Glossary

The General Armorial — the collection of officially confirmed coats of arms of noble families of the Russian Empire, established in 1797.

The genealogical register — a governorate registry of noble families, divided into six parts by the origin of title.

The Heraldry Office (Geroldiya) — the state body responsible for matters of the nobility and the confirmation of coats of arms and genealogies.

Letters patent for a title — an imperial document recording the grant of princely, comital or baronial rank.

Blazon — the heraldic description of a coat of arms in a specialised professional language, recording the colours, charges and their placement on the shield.

Deed (krepostnoi akt) — a document on the purchase, sale or transfer of real property, including ancestral estates.

Service record (formulyarny spisok) — an official document recording the career of a military or civil official.

Odnodvortsy — descendants of the service people of the southern frontiers of the Muscovite state, formally close to the nobility but in practice living a peasant way of life.

The Noble Deputy Assembly — the governorate body of noble self-government, which examined cases for entry into the genealogical register.
