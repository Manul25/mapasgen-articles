Surrogacy for Gay Couples in Europe: How the Laws Differ Across Countries

Europe has no unified approach to surrogacy at all, and even less so to surrogacy for same-sex couples: the continent's legal landscape looks less like variations on a single rule than a patchwork of mutually incompatible systems.

For gay couples considering surrogacy, this isn't an abstract detail but a factor that shapes the entire process: the country where donor material will be used and the child carried, the couple's country of residence, and the country where parental rights will need to be legally established — questions that need to be worked out long before the first contact with a clinic.

§ 02

A legal patchwork: who allows what

Most continental European countries — France, Germany, Italy, Spain — ban surrogacy outright, regardless of the couple's composition or whether it's commercial or altruistic. A surrogacy agreement in these jurisdictions is considered legally void, and in some cases even arranging the procedure abroad for one's own citizens borders on a legal grey zone upon returning home.

The UK represents a middle model: altruistic surrogacy is permitted (the surrogate can be reimbursed for expenses but not paid a fee), but obtaining parental rights requires a separate court order — a parental order — issued after the child is born, and by default the surrogate is initially considered the legal mother regardless of genetic relatedness. Greece and Portugal allow surrogacy in limited circumstances, but both countries have historically required residency and/or proven medical necessity, which in practice has made access difficult or impossible for foreign same-sex couples.

§ 03

Why Ukraine and Georgia were never an option for gay couples

Before 2022, Ukraine was considered one of the most affordable surrogacy destinations in the world, but Ukrainian law restricts the right to surrogacy to heterosexual married couples with medical grounds — meaning it never, even in theory, extended to same-sex couples, regardless of citizenship. That restriction still stands: Ukraine's very popularity as a destination in public discourse often created a mistaken impression of accessibility among couples from other countries, an impression that never reflected reality for same-sex partners.

A similar situation unfolded in Georgia, another popular destination for heterosexual foreign couples: the country allowed surrogacy for foreigners under terms also restricted to heterosexual couples, and in 2023 Georgian law went further and banned surrogacy for foreign nationals entirely, regardless of the couple's composition, closing the destination completely.

§ 04

Why European gay couples most often end up going overseas

As a result of these legal restrictions across the continent, most European gay couples planning surrogacy turn to programs in the US — above all in states with the most favorable laws, such as California, where surrogacy for same-sex couples is permitted on equal terms with any other couple, and parental rights for both partners can be established even before the child is born through a pre-birth order.

The flip side of this accessibility is cost: surrogacy programs in the US typically run from $150,000 to over $200,000, several times the cost of programs previously available, for instance, in Ukraine for heterosexual couples. For many gay couples in Europe, it's precisely this cost gap, not a lack of desire, that becomes the main obstacle on the path to parenthood through surrogacy.

§ 05

What happens after the child is born: the cross-border recognition problem

Legally establishing parental rights in the country where the child is born is only half the task. The other half is having those rights recognized in the country the family returns to, and this is where one of the most complex practical questions of the entire process arises: far from every European country automatically recognizes parental rights established abroad through surrogacy, especially if surrogacy itself is banned in that country.

A key precedent in this area was the European Court of Human Rights' 2014 ruling in Mennesson v. France: the court held that France's refusal to recognize the legal bond between a child and their genetic father, born through surrogacy abroad, violated Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights, protecting the right to respect for the child's private life. The ruling obligated France, and on the strength of this precedent, other Council of Europe states as well, to recognize a child's connection to at least their genetic parent, even where the surrogacy procedure itself is banned domestically — but this is far from the same as automatic recognition of parental rights for both partners in a couple.

How individual countries have applied this precedent varies widely in practice. Germany, for example, formally bans surrogacy, but following a 2014 ruling by the country's Federal Court of Justice, recognizes parental relationships established abroad as long as at least one partner is the child's genetic parent — provided the program took place in a country where surrogacy is legal. Switzerland, by contrast, long refused to recognize parental relationships even with a genetic parent if the child was born through surrogacy abroad, and is only gradually softening that position under the influence of European Court case law.

§ 06

Genetic parent and partner: two different legal statuses

Even where the Mennesson ruling and similar case law secure recognition of the bond with the child's genetic parent, the second partner in the couple — who has no genetic connection to the child — is in many countries required to go through a separate, often lengthy stepchild adoption process to obtain full parental rights. In some jurisdictions this process takes months or years, and during that time leaves the child legally connected to only one of their two actual parents.

This asymmetry creates practical risks that go beyond bureaucratic inconvenience: if the genetic parent is unable, for whatever reason, to carry out parental duties, and the second partner's adoption hasn't yet been completed, the child's legal status and their relationship with the second parent can become highly precarious. That's exactly why specialists in international family law recommend starting the second partner's adoption process as early as possible, alongside other legal steps, rather than putting it off for later.

§ 07

The dual selection: an egg donor and a surrogate

The practical side of surrogacy for a gay couple is more complex than for a heterosexual couple using surrogacy on medical grounds: the partners need not only to find a surrogate willing to carry the child, but also to separately select an egg donor, since the couple simply has no eggs of their own. This means two parallel selection processes, two sets of legal agreements, and typically a longer overall program timeline — from selecting a donor and surrogate to the child's birth usually takes one and a half to two and a half years.

A separate practical question is whether the partners match the egg donor and the surrogate as the same person or as two different people. In most modern programs, these roles are deliberately kept separate: combining genetic relatedness with carrying the pregnancy is considered to create a more complex psychological and legal situation, whereas separating the roles reduces both emotional and legal risk for everyone involved, including the surrogate herself.

§ 08

Growing alternatives: Canada and new destinations

Against the backdrop of Ukraine and Georgia closing to same-sex couples and the high cost of US programs, some European couples are turning to Canada, where altruistic surrogacy has been permitted for couples of any composition at the federal level since 2004. Canadian programs are typically less expensive than American ones, though direct payment to the surrogate beyond expense reimbursement is likewise banned there, which limits the number of women willing to participate in a program and lengthens waiting times.

At the same time, less established destinations are emerging in Latin America — Colombia and certain Mexican states, where the law either permits surrogacy without an explicit ban for same-sex couples, or doesn't regulate the field clearly enough. Specialists in international reproductive law generally advise treating such destinations with extra caution: the absence of an explicit ban isn't the same as clear legal protection, and an unregulated market increases risk for both parents and surrogates.

§ 10

When professional help is needed

We deliberately limit ourselves here to general patterns rather than legal advice for your situation. Questions related to choosing a jurisdiction, the legal status of both partners, and recognition of parental rights upon returning home are a reason to consult a lawyer specializing specifically in international reproductive and family law, not a general-practice lawyer: the law in this field changes quickly and varies significantly between countries.

§ 11

The bottom line

Surrogacy for gay couples in Europe isn't a single path but a series of legal forks, at each of which the rules change from country to country, and sometimes from year to year: destinations that seemed accessible not long ago close by law, and international court rulings gradually, though not completely, even out the legal standing of children born through surrogacy across different jurisdictions.

The practical takeaway for couples considering this path: the legal questions — the country where the program takes place, recognition of parental rights, the second partner's legal status — need to be clarified before, not after, starting the process, and this should be done with the help of an international family law specialist, not by relying on other couples' experience.

Glossary

  • Altruistic surrogacy — a model in which the surrogate is reimbursed for expenses but not paid a fee for the service itself, as distinct from commercial surrogacy.
  • Parental order — a court order in the UK and several other jurisdictions establishing a parent's rights over a child born through surrogacy, issued after the child's birth.
  • Pre-birth order — a court ruling establishing parental rights before the child is even born, common in certain US states.
  • Mennesson v. France — a 2014 European Court of Human Rights ruling obligating states to recognize a child's legal connection to at least their genetic parent when born through surrogacy abroad.
  • Stepchild adoption — the legal procedure for a partner with no genetic connection to the child to obtain parental rights, used in many countries for the second parent in a same-sex couple.
