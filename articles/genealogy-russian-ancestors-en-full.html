§ 01

In 1666–1667, a church council met in Moscow and, for the first time in Russia, formally raised the question of keeping records of parishioners' births, marriages, and deaths. The matter, however, stayed mostly talk for a long while: in 1702, Peter the Great issued a separate decree requiring Moscow priests to submit weekly reports on births and deaths to the Patriarchal Spiritual Office, but even that never became standard practice. It wasn't until May 1722, in an addendum to the Spiritual Regulation, that a requirement finally took hold: every parish in the empire was now obliged to keep parish registers. Modern genealogists treat this date as a convenient starting point — from 1722 onward, written traces of the ancestors of people who lived in the Russian Empire become genuinely abundant, regardless of which people, social class, or faith a given family belonged to.

More than three centuries have passed since then, along with two changes of political system, several wars, and waves of emigration — and today, documents about one's ancestors can be sitting almost anywhere: in a regional archive covering a former province, in the holdings of a central Moscow archive, on a microfilm shot by Mormon missionaries during the Soviet era, or in the police prefecture of a French town where an émigré fleeing the revolution once settled. This article is a detailed, practical breakdown of how this whole system of sources works, what documents exist for different eras and social groups, and who to contact at each stage of the search.

§ 02

Start with Conversations in the Family

Before storming the archives, it's worth exhausting the most accessible source of all: the memory of living relatives. The names of great-grandparents, village names, occupations, stories about moves, old letters, photographs with captions on the back, military service records, marriage certificates — all of this sharply narrows down the archival search. Soviet-era documents are especially valuable, since they often state a place of birth, which immediately points to which regional archive might hold earlier parish records. Don't dismiss oral details that seem trivial at first glance, either: a remark like "our family was all from somewhere near Tula" or "grandfather said he was born into an Old Believer family" is already a search direction and a hint at which type of document you'll need.

§ 03

Parish Registers: The Main Source Before 1918

A parish register (metricheskaya kniga) was a document kept in every Orthodox parish, divided into three sections: births, marriages, and deaths. A birth entry typically recorded the date of birth and baptism, the infant's name, the names and social status of the parents, and the names of godparents — who were often relatives or close neighbours of the family and can themselves serve as a good lead for further research. A marriage entry included the names and ages of the bride and groom, their social class, which marriage this was for each of them (first, second, and so on), and the names of witnesses. A death entry recorded the deceased's age, cause of death (often imprecise, phrased as "of fever" or "of old age"), and place of burial.

The registers were kept in two copies: one stayed at the church itself, the other was copied out each year and sent for safekeeping to the consistory — the administrative body of the diocese. This matters in practice: if a particular church's parish copy hasn't survived (and after the revolution, wars, and simple time, a great deal hasn't), there's a chance the consistory copy survived — and the reverse is also true: sometimes only the parish copy made it through.

Non-Orthodox peoples of the empire had their own arrangements. Old Believers only gained the right to keep their own registers after an 1905 law — before that, they were registered by secular district administrations. Records for Muslims were kept by mullahs at mosques, for Jews by rabbis or specially appointed community scribes, and for Lutherans by pastors, and weren't always written in Russian. If your ancestors belonged to one of these groups, you'll need to look not only in consistory holdings but also in district administration records, the registers of the relevant religious community, or, for Jews of the Russian Empire, in specialized databases such as JewishGen, which gather precisely these records in one place.

Good news for anyone starting a search today: a substantial share of parish registers has already been digitized. In the mid-twentieth century, Mormon missions carried out large-scale microfilming of archives worldwide, including Soviet ones, and today that material is freely available on FamilySearch. You can check the survival catalogue for registers held in Russian, Ukrainian, and Belarusian archives on the specialized site metrics.tilda.ws, while the originals generally remain at the regional state archive covering the province where your ancestors lived — and modern archives are increasingly posting scans in their own information systems.

§ 04

Confession Lists: A Backup Source When Registers Are Missing

Besides parish registers, churches also drew up another annual document: the confession list, or ispovednaya rospis'. A standard format for these was introduced by a Synod decree in 1737 and remained almost unchanged until 1917, with some places continuing to compile them even into the 1920s. Unlike a parish register, which records individual events, a confession list is essentially an annual census of every parishioner in the entire parish, organized by household and family, noting who confessed and took communion that year and who didn't, and why. For a genealogist, this is an invaluable source: you can see a family's full composition in a given year even if none of its members happened to be born or die that particular year — and so wouldn't appear in the parish register at all. Confession lists have survived less well, on average, than parish registers, but that's exactly why it's worth checking both sources in parallel: what's missing from one sometimes turns up in the other.

§ 05

Revision Lists and Censuses: Records Kept for Tax Purposes

Alongside church record-keeping, Russia ran a separate, secular system of accounting from 1718 onward: the revisions, or censuses of the taxable population for tax purposes. The unit of account was the "revision soul," and information on each household was entered into a document called a revision list (revizskaya skazka): the name, patronymic, and surname of the head of household, the age and composition of the family, each member's relation to the head, and — before the abolition of serfdom — which landowner the peasants belonged to. Between revisions, notes were added on the fate of those absent: died, fled, resettled, drafted as a soldier — entries that often serve as the only clue to what happened to a person afterward. Ten revisions took place in total between 1718 and 1858, and records from the earliest ones are held at the Russian State Archive of Ancient Acts in Moscow.

Revision lists are especially important for those whose ancestors weren't part of Orthodox church record-keeping at all — Muslims, pagans, members of smaller peoples of the empire — and also for serfs, whose attachment to a specific landowner often provides an additional lead: estate archives and the correspondence of landowning families sometimes preserve information about peasant households more precisely than state archives do. In addition, Russia conducted its first general population census in 1897 — modern in structure, with an individual census form for every person, recording age, literacy, occupation, and religion. Unfortunately, the survival of 1897 census forms varies enormously by region: some provinces' records survived almost intact, others only in fragments, but wherever they did survive, this is one of the richest sources available.

§ 06

An Important Practical Detail: Search by Province, Not by the Modern Map

One of the most common mistakes beginning researchers make is trying to locate an archive based on a "modern address." The administrative structure of the Russian Empire was organized into provinces (guberniyas) and districts (uyezds), and their borders almost never match those of today's regions and districts in Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, or the Baltic states. The same settlement might have changed province, then district, and then country over its history, and archival holdings were organized precisely around those pre-revolutionary administrative units — so that's exactly how you need to search.

Specialized mapping services that solve exactly this problem are a huge help here: they overlay old provincial maps onto a modern satellite or schematic map, letting you see which province and district a given point on today's map corresponds to — and, conversely, find a village on an old map that's known today by a different name or no longer exists at all. The best-known and most convenient such resource in the Russian-speaking world is EtoMesto.ru: you can search for settlements by name (including vanished ones), adjust the transparency of the old map laid over the modern one, and flip through dozens of historical maps from different eras — from eighteenth-century general land-survey plans and the mid-nineteenth-century Schubert and Mende maps to Soviet-era military topographic maps from the General Staff. Retromap.ru serves a similar function and is especially convenient for comparison: you can open an old map and a modern one side by side, in two synchronized windows, and study the same territory across different eras at once. Before writing to an archive, it's worth first opening one of these services, locating the village in question on the old map, and pinpointing exactly which consistory's or district's holdings you need to request.

§ 07

Surnames Are Newer Than You Might Think

Another quirk worth knowing in advance: stable, hereditary surnames among the peasant majority of the Russian Empire became fixed much later than they did among the nobility, clergy, and merchant class. The upper classes already had surnames by the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, while peasants long appeared in documents under a first name and patronymic, or a local nickname that could change from one generation to the next. Permanent surnames became fixed for peasants in waves throughout the second half of the nineteenth century, and the process was only finally completed during the Soviet era, in the course of the 1930s passportization campaign. The practical consequence: the further back into the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries a search goes, the more likely it is that the same family will appear in different documents under different "surnames" — really, nicknames that hadn't yet become permanent. This isn't a clerk's error and not a reason to assume the line has broken; it's a normal feature of the sources, and the only way around it is to match records not by surname, but by the combination of first name, patronymic, age, family composition, and place of residence.

§ 08

The 1917–1918 Divide: A New Record-Keeping System

The revolution split the history of Russian population record-keeping into two entirely different worlds. From 1918 onward, parish registers were replaced by civil status acts, kept not by churches but by Soviet civil registry offices — ZAGS. A portion of parish registers from the earlier period continued to be physically held at ZAGS offices, rather than archives, for a long time, and were only transferred to state archives gradually, sometimes as late as the 2000s — worth keeping in mind if an archive tells you it has no relevant records: they may still be sitting at the local ZAGS office, or may have been handed over relatively recently.

The Soviet period also brought an entirely new type of document unfamiliar to pre-revolutionary genealogy: records related to political repression. If an ancestor was caught up in the repressions, it's worth searching open databases maintained by human-rights and historical-education organizations, in particular the "Open List" project. Access to part of the security services' archival files is also gradually opening up to direct relatives upon official request, though the procedure and timelines vary considerably by archive and region. House registers and personnel files kept at the workplace, sometimes preserved in the departmental archives of enterprises and institutions, remain useful Soviet-era sources too.

§ 09

If Your Ancestors Emigrated: Three Waves, Three Different Sets of Archives

Emigration from Russia and the USSR after 1917 is usually divided by researchers into three waves, and for genealogical purposes it matters which wave a particular family belongs to, because each wave has its own set of archives. The first wave, the so-called White emigration, spans 1917 to the late 1920s: by various estimates, somewhere between one and three million people left the country during this period — military and civilian figures, clergy, nobility, and intelligentsia fleeing Soviet rule and famine. The main centres of this emigration were Berlin, Prague, Belgrade, Constantinople, Harbin, and, of course, Paris. The second wave is tied to the final years of and immediately after the Second World War — the so-called displaced persons, who ended up in refugee camps in Germany and Austria before scattering to various countries. The third wave is the emigration from the USSR in the 1970s and 1980s, largely connected to exit permits granted to Jews, headed mostly for Israel, the United States, and a handful of Western European countries.

This part of the story matters especially to those searching for Russian roots while living in France today — and such searches aren't a coincidence: it was the first wave of emigration that ultimately made Paris one of Europe's main centres of the Russian diaspora for several decades, complete with its own newspapers, churches, the cemetery at Sainte-Geneviève-des-Bois, and a whole infrastructure of "Russian Paris." Refugees from the former empire formally found themselves stateless — the Bolshevik government had stripped them of citizenship, and there was no longer anyone willing to recognize them as subjects of the Russian Empire. Norwegian polar explorer and diplomat Fridtjof Nansen found a solution: in 1922, under the auspices of the League of Nations, a special document was introduced known as the "Nansen passport" — an identity document for stateless people that let them legally live and travel across Europe.

§ 10

If You're French with Russian Roots: Where to Start, Specifically, in France

Hundreds of thousands of descendants of that first emigration wave live in France today, and for most of them the search works in mirror image compared to what's described above: not "from Russia to France," but "from France to Russia." The logic here is straightforward: the closer a document is to our own time, the more accessible and complete it tends to be, so it makes sense to start on the French side and work backward to the moment the border was crossed — and only then head toward Russia.

The first and most reliable step is to find French civil status records (actes d'état civil) for a great-grandparent or earlier ancestor: a birth, marriage, or death certificate issued by a French town hall. These are requested at the town hall where the event took place or at the departmental archive (archives départementales), and they almost always state the exact place and date of birth — and, for marriage records, the names and origins of both sets of parents as well. If an ancestor was born not in Russia but into an émigré family already in France, their own birth certificate will almost certainly state where their parents were born — exactly the lead that points back to Russia.

From there, it's worth turning to specifically émigré sources, of which France has preserved a surprising number. The French Office for the Protection of Refugees and Stateless Persons (OFPRA) has digitized and made publicly available, on its own archival portal, the personal files of refugees granted protection between 1924 and 1952 — these are, above all, Nansen-passport holders: Russians, Georgians, and Armenians. Access requires free registration; part of the collection has already been indexed by name, part hasn't yet, but even partial indexing makes those 250-plus boxes of archival material a real, working source rather than a theoretical possibility. Some quite well-known stories turn up among the digitized files — including, for instance, the personal file of Vera Obolenskaya, the future heroine of the French Resistance, who arrived in France precisely as a Nansen-passport holder.

In parallel, it's worth checking two avenues within French state archives. First, the French National Archives (Archives nationales) hold naturalization files in the BB/11 series — this is where the paperwork went for those émigrés who eventually obtained French citizenship, while the actual naturalization decisions were published in the official Journal officiel. Second, prefecture and departmental archives kept a personal record card — fichier des étrangers — on every foreigner residing in France, noting their date and place of birth, their route of entry into the country, and their residence permits, renewed year after year. Many first-wave émigrés entered through Cherbourg or Marseille, and port registration logs are also sometimes preserved in the archives of the corresponding departments.

A separate and very rich layer of sources is tied to the Russian Orthodox Church in emigration. The Cathedral of Saint Alexander Nevsky on Rue Daru in Paris kept its own parish registers for decades — records of baptisms, marriages, and funerals for émigré parishioners, following much the same logic as pre-revolutionary parish registers — and these records often turn out to be the only written trace of an event that, legally speaking, took place outside Russia altogether. The cemetery at Sainte-Geneviève-des-Bois near Paris, which became the main necropolis of the Russian emigration, keeps burial registers, and the inscriptions on the gravestones themselves frequently state pre-revolutionary ranks, titles, or place of birth in Russia — an inexpensive and accessible way to at least partially reconstruct a biography without ever visiting an archive.

Finally, the émigré press of that period — newspapers published in Paris such as Latest News (Poslednie Novosti) and Renaissance (Vozrozhdenie) — printed obituaries and announcements of weddings and anniversaries that often include full names and even details of a family's background. A substantial share of these publications has been digitized and is available through Gallica, the portal of the French National Library, and a full-text search by surname sometimes turns up a surprisingly detailed result exactly where an official archival inquiry came up empty.

Once French documents reveal a specific Russian place of birth and at least an approximate year, the search reverts to the logic described earlier in this article: the province and district, the parish registers or confession lists of the relevant parish, and revision lists if you're dealing with earlier generations. In other words, the French leg of the journey usually doesn't replace the Russian archival search — it serves as a bridge to it. But without that bridge, a great many researchers simply have no idea which Russian region to start with.

§ 11

If You're German with Russian Roots: The Story of the Volga and Black Sea Germans

German descendants of settlers from Russia have an entirely separate story of their own, and it begins not with emigration but with an invitation. On December 4, 1762, Catherine the Great — herself German by birth — signed a manifesto permitting foreigners to settle in Russia, and on July 22, 1763, a second, far more detailed manifesto followed, spelling out the specific terms: freedom of religion, full self-governance for the colonies without interference from officials, exemption from taxes for thirty years and from military service, and interest-free loans to help settlers get established. Around 30,000 people moved to Russia over the following three years, mostly from German lands devastated by the Seven Years' War, above all from Hesse. The colonists settled along the Volga and, later, on the shores of the Black Sea, preserving their German language, Lutheran or Catholic faith, and way of life almost unchanged for more than a century — so isolated that their dialect still surprises linguists today as a kind of preserved eighteenth-century German.

The colonists' privileges began unraveling after Alexander II's reforms: from 1871, German settlers lost their special status and became subject to military service like the rest of the empire's population. After the 1917 revolution, Soviet rule, by contrast, briefly granted the Volga Germans territorial autonomy — in 1924, the Volga German ASSR was established, with its capital at Engels. That autonomy ended abruptly and tragically on August 28, 1941, when a decree ordered the resettlement of the Volga Germans: the republic was dissolved, and nearly 900,000 people were deported to Siberia and Kazakhstan, many of them through the so-called labour army — in effect, forced-labour camps. A 1948 decree fixed the deported Germans' status as permanent, with no right to return to their former places of residence; the formal ban was only eased in 1955, and even then, returning to the Volga region remained, in practice, nearly impossible.

It was precisely this deportation that scattered millions of people of German descent across Kazakhstan, Siberia, and the Urals — and it was from there, not from the pre-war Volga region, that a mass exodus to Germany began in the 1990s. After the collapse of the USSR, Germany's law on resettlers (Bundesvertriebenengesetz) gave ethnic Germans and their family members the right to move to Germany with the status of late resettlers — Spätaussiedler — and tens of thousands of families whose ancestors had come to Russia two centuries earlier at Catherine the Great's invitation ultimately returned to their ancestors' country of origin, now as its citizens.

This history carries several practical consequences for genealogical research. First, the German federal administrative office (Bundesverwaltungsamt), which processed late-resettler applications, opened a personal file on every applicant containing proof of German ancestry — in effect, a ready-made genealogical dossier with names, dates, and places of birth across several generations; if an older relative went through this procedure, it's worth finding out whether their file has survived. Second, during the Second World War, Nazi authorities in occupied territories ran their own ancestry-verification process through the so-called Einwandererzentralstelle (EWZ) — and the personal files from that procedure, for all the grimness of their historical context, are preserved today in archives (including the Bundesarchiv and the Arolsen Archives) and often contain detailed genealogical information going back several generations, gathered specifically for racial screening purposes.

Third, the specialized archive for the Volga German colonies specifically is the State Historical Archive of the Volga Germans in Engels, which holds colony documents from 1767 to 1941, including Lutheran and Catholic parish registers — these were kept separately from Orthodox consistories among the colonists, often in German. An active, sizeable Russian- and German-speaking community of researchers gathers on the Wolgadeutsche.net forum, where you can find not just advice but already-scanned documents for specific colonies. For German Mennonites, many of whom settled on the Black Sea and in the southern steppe colonies, a separate major resource is the specialized Mennonite genealogical database GRANDMA, which exists precisely because Mennonite communities traditionally kept particularly detailed records of kinship within the community.

§ 12

DNA Tests as an Additional Tool

When the archival trail goes cold, genetic genealogy can help. Commercial DNA tests don't replace archival documents, but they can confirm or call into question a relationship, surface previously unknown relatives through matches in shared databases, and sometimes point to an approximate region of origin along both lines of ancestry. For Eastern European and Russian ancestry, large international databases with a substantial number of users from the region are especially useful — DNA matches become more informative the more people from that population have already been tested.

That's precisely why the Genetik Hub section on MAPASGEN brings together genetics and ancestry in one place: a DNA test gives you a starting point — an approximate region and a circle of possible relatives — and from there, archival research through parish registers, confession lists, and revision lists turns that starting point into actual names, dates, and villages. Neither works nearly as well alone: DNA without archives stays a set of ancestry percentages, while archives without DNA sometimes run into a break in the documentary chain that a genetic match with a distant relative can unexpectedly help close.

§ 15

Where to Turn: A Practical List

Researching ancestors from the Russian Empire almost never turns into a straightforward, linear process: more often, it resembles the work of a detective piecing together a picture from scattered fragments — a church record here, a tax list there, a tattered French file on an émigré somewhere else entirely. But precisely because this part of the world kept written records of its population for three centuries straight, through every upheaval, the picture almost always has its missing pieces somewhere — you just need to know which layer of history to look in, and on which map to search for a place that, on today's map, may no longer exist at all.
