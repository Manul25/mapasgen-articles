Repressions and the Gulag: How to Find Information About a Repressed Ancestor

In 2007, search volunteers at the Butovo firing range outside Moscow — one of the largest sites of mass execution in the late 1930s — completed work that had taken nearly a decade: establishing the names of 20,762 people shot there in 1937–1938 and buried in unmarked trenches. Until that point, many families had spent years receiving the formulaic official response 'sentenced to ten years without the right of correspondence' — a phrase that, in reality, meant execution within days of the sentence, but which was understood literally for decades, with families waiting for word from someone with whom correspondence was physically impossible because he was already dead. Butovo's story is a single, but telling, example of how methodical archival work can give families back not just the fact of a death, but a specific place and date that never appeared on any official notice.

In 1992, the President of Russia signed the Law 'On the Rehabilitation of Victims of Political Repression', which for the first time established, at the state level, the right of citizens and their descendants to examine the archival investigation files of their repressed relatives. Before that point, access to such documents had been all but impossible: the files were kept classified in the archives of the state security agencies, and the subject of repression itself remained taboo for decades, even within private family conversations. The 1992 law didn't just open the archives — it created the legal foundation without which millions of Russian families would never have learned what actually happened to their grandparents and great-grandparents in the 1930s through the 1950s.

Why Sort Out the Terminology Before Searching

In everyday speech, the word 'repression' covers any form of Soviet-era state violence, but the archival system distinguishes between cases by their specific legal basis, and that determines which agency to search. Execution or a camp sentence under a political article was typically a case handled by the state security agencies (the Cheka-OGPU-NKVD-KGB, under their successive names). Dekulakisation and the expulsion of peasant families in the early 1930s was not a criminal case at all but an administrative expulsion, whose documents may be held in the archives of the Interior Ministry and regional archives at the place of exile. The deportation of entire peoples — Crimean Tatars, Chechens, Volga Germans, Koreans and others — is a separate category, with its own system of recording special settlers. Understanding which of these categories a particular case falls into saves months of fruitless requests to the wrong agency.

The Archival Investigation File: What to Look For Inside

The archival investigation file (arkhivno-sledstvennoe delo) is the core document for cases handled by the state security agencies, and it almost always contains far more information than relatives expect, having braced themselves for nothing but a dry sentence. A typical file includes the arrest report listing the home address and family composition at the time of arrest, a questionnaire of the arrested person with biographical details, interrogation transcripts, the indictment, and the sentence itself — whether execution, a camp term, or exile. For a relative, this is often the only surviving source recording what the family looked like immediately before the catastrophe: who worked where, who was studying, what the address was. As a rule, only a direct relative of the rehabilitated person — a child, grandchild, or spouse — can obtain a copy of the file, by applying directly to the regional FSB or Interior Ministry directorate holding the file, or through the prosecutor's office for the region where the rehabilitation decision was issued.

Memorial: A Civic Database That Grew Out of Samizdat

The International Memorial Society, founded in 1989 in the late Soviet period of perestroika, set out from the very beginning not merely to research the subject but to systematically gather the names of victims of political repression — a task the state archives alone could not, and did not always want to, undertake. The result was the 'Victims of Political Terror in the USSR' database, which consolidated information from dozens of regional Books of Memory published across the country from the 1990s onward, and which today contains millions of names along with year of birth, place of residence, date of arrest, and fate. This is the first and fastest step in any search: before sending an official request to an FSB archive, it makes sense to check the surname in the Memorial database, since a match there immediately supplies the case number, the agency that issued the sentence, and the exact date of events — all needed for the subsequent official request.

The Gulag: A System That Can Also Be Mapped

The acronym GULAG — Main Directorate of Camps — refers not to a single camp but to a centralised system that administered hundreds of corrective-labour camps and colonies across the entire territory of the USSR from 1930 to 1960. Each individual camp kept its own records, and a prisoner's personal file, after release, transfer, or death, may have remained in the archive of that specific camp rather than in any central archive. To trace the fate of someone who served a sentence, it's important to establish not just the fact of conviction but the specific camp or camp system (for example, Vorkutlag, Norillag, Kolyma) to which they were sent — this information is usually found within the archival investigation file itself, or in a later prisoner's registration file, which may have survived in the archive of the regional Federal Penitentiary Service directorate (GUFSIN).

Special Settlers: Deportation as a Separate Record-Keeping System

The fate of deported peoples and dekulakised peasants was recorded not by the investigative agencies but by special NKVD commandant's offices, which kept registration cards for special settlers — a distinct type of document, neither a criminal case nor a camp personal file. These cards listed family composition, the date of arrival at the special settlement, the specific settlement itself, and frequently included notations of deaths, births, and movements within the special settlement system over many years. Such documents are held today mainly at the State Archive of the Russian Federation (GARF) and in the archives of the regional Interior Ministry directorates for the place to which the family was sent — and since deportation always affected an entire family rather than a single individual, a special settler's card that turns up almost always provides information about several relatives at once.

Rehabilitation: The Certificate That Unlocks Access to the File

Most victims of political repression were rehabilitated — meaning their sentence was officially recognised as unlawful — either during the brief rehabilitation campaign of the 1950s following Stalin's death, or later, in the post-Soviet period, under the 1991–1992 law. The rehabilitation certificate is a separate document issued by the prosecutor's office, and it often becomes the first thread to pull in order to request the archival investigation file itself. If rehabilitation took place in the 1950s, the documents relating to it may be held separately from the main investigation file, and sometimes a rehabilitation certificate preserved in a family archive turns out to be the only lead worth starting an official request from.

Children of the Repressed: ChSIR and Special Orphanages

Repression rarely stopped at a single person: the wife of someone convicted under a political article frequently received her own sentence under the special category of ChSIR — 'member of the family of a traitor to the motherland' — introduced by an NKVD order in 1937 and applied chiefly to the wives of repressed military and party figures. ChSIR cases are held in the same state security archives as the cases of those originally convicted, but constitute separate investigative proceedings that must be requested separately, even where a husband and wife were essentially convicted over the same event. Children left without either parent were frequently sent to special orphanages for children of 'enemies of the people', where a child could be given a new surname and have any information about their real parents concealed — a separate and deeply painful category of documentation that explains why, in some families, the connection between generations was physically severed: a person who grew up in such an orphanage sometimes didn't learn their real surname until receiving an archival certificate as an adult. Information about a child's stay in such an institution needs to be sought in the regional archives of education or social welfare authorities for the place where the institution was located, not in the files of the state security agencies.

The Open List: A Crowdsourced Complement to Memorial

Alongside the Memorial database, since 2016 a project called the Open List (Otkrytyi Spisok) has been developing — a less well-known but actively growing crowdsourced database where any user can not only search for names but also add new information, photographs and documents about repressed relatives, drawing on archival certificates they've already obtained. Unlike Memorial, which mainly consolidates data from regional Books of Memory, the Open List is built as an open, editable database, similar in logic to a wiki, and frequently contains information added by descendants after their own successful archival request — meaning it can include details absent even from the official Book of Memory for a given region.

Five Mistakes When Searching for a Repressed Ancestor

  • Sending a request straight to the central FSB archive in Moscow without first establishing which region the case was handled in.
  • Confusing a political prisoner's archival investigation file with a Gulag prisoner's personal file — these are different documents held in different archives.
  • Skipping the Memorial database before an official request, and losing time searching without a case number.
  • Treating dekulakisation or deportation as a criminal case and searching the state security agencies instead of the Interior Ministry archives and GARF.
  • Overlooking the fact that the rehabilitation certificate and the archival investigation file may be held separately and require two distinct requests.

Search Checklist

  • Determine the category: political conviction, camp sentence, dekulakisation, or the deportation of an entire people.
  • Check your ancestor's surname in the Memorial database to obtain a case number and the agency that issued the ruling.
  • For a political case, send a request to the regional FSB directorate or prosecutor's office for the region where the investigation is presumed to have taken place.
  • For a camp sentence, identify the specific camp and request the prisoner's personal file from the relevant regional GUFSIN archive.
  • For deportation or dekulakisation, contact GARF and the regional Interior Ministry archive for the place of special settlement.
  • If the family has kept a rehabilitation certificate, use the details it contains as the starting point for requesting the full file.

Researching a repressed ancestor almost never fits into a single request: the Soviet system for recording repression was organised by separate agencies, and the fate of a single person might be recorded across several archives at once — an investigation file held by the state security agencies, a camp personal file, a special settler's card, a rehabilitation certificate. A systematic understanding of this structure turns the search from guesswork into a sequence of specific, well-grounded requests.

Glossary

Archival investigation file (arkhivno-sledstvennoe delo) — the core document of the state security agencies for a case of political conviction, including interrogation transcripts and the sentence.

Gulag — the Main Directorate of Camps, the USSR's centralised system of corrective-labour camps and colonies from 1930 to 1960.

Special settler — a person deported or administratively expelled and registered with an NKVD special commandant's office.

Rehabilitation — the official recognition of a sentence as unlawful, formalised by a prosecutor's certificate.

International Memorial Society — a civic organisation founded in 1989, maintaining a database of victims of political repression in the USSR.

ChSIR — 'member of the family of a traitor to the motherland', a category of conviction applied to the wives of the repressed, introduced by an NKVD order in 1937.

The Open List (Otkrytyi Spisok) — a crowdsourced database of victims of political repression, in existence since 2016.

GARF — the State Archive of the Russian Federation, holding a substantial share of documents on deportations and special settlements.
