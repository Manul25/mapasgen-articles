Section VI

VI. Writing directly to archives

Direct contact with an archive opens access to material not found in any online database. Each country has its own record-keeping system, language of official correspondence, timelines and fees.

Three types of archives in Europe

State archives — civil acts, censuses, tax records, military lists, court documents.

Church archives — vital records before civil registration. In Germany: Bistumsarchiv (Catholic), Landeskirchliches Archiv (Lutheran). Often independent from state archives.

Municipal archives (Stadtarchiv, Archivio storico comunale) — town council records, resident lists, professional registers.

Before writing a request

Make sure you have: exact name of ancestor (all spelling variants), approximate years of life (±10 years), specific locality, religion (this determines which archive — state or church). Without this data the archive cannot conduct a search.

If the archive does not reply

Wait at least 3 months before a follow-up. In the follow-up letter, refer to the first (state the date sent). If silence continues — try contacting by telephone. As a last resort, hire a local genealogist for a personal visit.

Section VII

VII. Reading historic records and church books

Finding a digitised document is half the work. The other half is reading it. Historic documents are written in multiple scripts, in multiple languages, with characteristic abbreviations.

Main types of records

Baptism record: date of baptism (not birth — they could differ by 1–3 days), child's name, parents' names (mother's maiden name), godparents' names. Godparents were often close relatives — this helps reconstruct social networks.

Marriage record: names of bride and groom with places of origin, date and location, names of witnesses. German records often noted status — widower/widow, first/second marriage.

Scripts and writing systems

Kurrent: Kurrent: handwritten Gothic script used in German-speaking areas from the 16th to mid-20th century. Completely unlike modern Latin handwriting.

Sütterlin: Sütterlin: simplified Kurrent, taught in German schools until 1941. Documents from the first half of the 20th century are often written in it.

Julian and Gregorian calendars: Orthodox documents use the "Old Style". In the 19th century the difference is 12 days, in the 20th — 13 days. Add 13 days to an Old Style date to get the Gregorian date.

Reading practice

Start with a document where you already know some details — this lets you calibrate the scribe's handwriting. One scribe kept a register for years: once you master their hand on one page, the entire volume becomes readable.

Section VIII

VIII. Country by country: full EU regional guide

Each country has its own archival system, its own timeframes for civil registration, its own key portals and specific challenges.

🇩🇪Germany

Civil registration 1874. Church books from 16th–17th c. Standesamt records under 110 yrs (births) / 80 yrs restricted. Key archives: Bundesarchiv, Landesarchive, Standesämter, Bistumsarchive, Landeskirchliche Archive.

For Volga Germans: Hauptarchiv der Rußlanddeutschen. For eastern provinces (Silesia, East Prussia): Standesamt Herne.

🇵🇱Poland

Three archival traditions by partition. Russian part: civil acts 1808 (Latin to 1826, Polish to 1868, Russian after). Prussian: Standesamt 1874. Austrian (Galicia): Latin church records from 1784.

Tools: szukajwarchiwach.gov.pl, geneteka.genealodzy.pl, AGAD Warsaw (pre-19th c.).

🇦🇹Austria / Czech Republic / Slovakia

Austria: registers from 16th c., civil registration only 1939. Digitised via Matricula. Czech Republic: ebadatelna.nacr.cz. Slovakia: Slovenský národný archív + FamilySearch.

🇮🇹Italy

Civil registration 1866. Records to 1910 via Antenati portal. Later records — request to Comune or Archivio di Stato. Mass emigration 1880–1930.

🇫🇷France / Belgium / Netherlands

France: civil registration 1792. Most files to 1902 digitised. francearchives.gouv.fr aggregates all regional archives. Netherlands: WieWasWie.nl covers collections to 1950.

🌊Estonia · Latvia · Lithuania

Complex heritage: records distributed between national archives, Polish, Russian and German archives (for Baltic Germans). Lithuania: LVIA — main source, archyvai.lt. Estonia: ra.ee. Latvia: lvva.lv.

☀️Spain · Portugal · Greece

Spain: civil registration 1871. Church books in Archivo Diocesano. FamilySearch: Catalonia, Aragon, Andalusia. Greece: Orthodox tradition, civil registration 1856. Portugal: digitarq.arquivos.pt.

Section IX

IX. DNA genealogy: advanced level

A genetic test is not a magic button. The result alone will not tell you who your ancestors were. But combined with documentary research it can break through a wall where archives are silent.

Three types of analysis

Autosomal test (atDNA): analyses DNA from all ancestors. Reliable to 5–6 generations back. The further the relationship, the less reliable.

Y-DNA test: passed strictly through the male line. Ideal for tracing the paternal line for centuries. Allows identification of a haplogroup and male relatives with the same surname.

Mitochondrial test (mtDNA): passed strictly through the female line. Useful for ethnic connections, but matches do not always indicate close kinship.

Cluster analysis: the AutoClustering tool groups matches by shared segments. Each cluster is one branch of your tree.

Endogamy: in communities with high endogamy (Ashkenazi Jews, isolated islands) standard cM values do not apply. Specialised tools exist for such cases.

The key principle

DNA points the direction, documents confirm the connection. No genetic test replaces archival evidence in a legal context.

Section X

X. Citizenship by descent: what you need to prove

The documentary chain you build through genealogical research can have direct legal consequences. In several EU countries, descendants of citizens have the right to citizenship — provided the connection is documented.

Legal disclaimer

Citizenship legislation changes. Information is current as of 2025. Before submitting documents, consult a lawyer specialising in citizenship for the specific country.

Overview of programmes

How a documentary chain is built

Any programme requires an unbroken chain of documents from you to the ancestor-citizen. Each link is a birth (or baptism) certificate, marriage certificate (if the surname changed) and a document confirming the ancestor's citizenship.

1
Establish the "entry point"

Identify the specific ancestor through whom you are claiming citizenship. Establish when and why they held the right to that citizenship.

2
Build the documentary chain

Every step from you to the entry point must be supported by a document. Gaps in the chain are grounds for refusal.

3
Apostille and translate documents

All foreign documents require an apostille and a notarially certified translation into the language of the country where you are applying.

4
Apply through the correct authority

Germany: BVA or embassy. Italy: consulate in your country of residence. Lithuania/Latvia: Department of Citizenship. Ireland: Ministry of Justice.

Genealogist or lawyer?

For complex cases: a certified genealogist (VdF or AGG in Germany, BCG in the USA) for building the chain, and a citizenship lawyer for preparing the application. This significantly reduces the risk of refusal.
