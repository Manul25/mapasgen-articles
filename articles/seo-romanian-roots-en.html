Romanian Roots: How to Search for Ancestors in a Country at the Crossroads of Four Empires

Modern Romania is a surprisingly young country, given the sheer volume of ancient archives tied to it: it has existed within its current borders only since 1918, and before that its four historical regions spent half a millennium living under four entirely different systems of population record-keeping. Moldavia and Wallachia were autonomous principalities under Istanbul's authority, keeping church registers in the Orthodox tradition; Transylvania answered to Budapest and Vienna and wrote in Hungarian and German; Bessarabia was a governorate of the Russian Empire; Northern Bukovina, after 1940, stopped being Romanian territory altogether. For genealogical research, this means one thing: before opening a single archival portal, you need to work out which of the four countries your ancestor actually lived in — formal membership in modern-day Romania explains almost nothing here.

Moldavia and Wallachia: From Ottoman Autonomy to Kingdom

The Danubian principalities of Moldavia and Wallachia paid tribute to the Ottoman sultan for centuries while retaining their own ruling princes (domnitori) and Orthodox church organisation. The two principalities united into a single state in 1859, following the election of a shared prince, Alexandru Ioan Cuza; full independence from the Ottoman Empire was recognised in 1878 after the Russo-Turkish War, and the country gained kingdom status in 1881. It was Cuza who carried out the 1865 reform that laid the groundwork for modern Romanian civil law — and that same reform marks the starting point for the civil registry discussed below.

Cyrillic for a Romance Language: An Alphabet That Changed Within a Single Generation

Until the mid-nineteenth century, Romanian — a Romance language — was written in the Cyrillic alphabet, a tradition inherited from the Church Slavonic script shared by the region's Orthodox principalities. The shift to the Latin alphabet began in the 1830s in printing and was finally cemented by administrative decrees of the 1860s. The practical consequence for a researcher: a register entry from 1840 and one from 1875, from the very same Moldavian parish, can look like two different languages, even though the underlying speech is identical. Reading Cyrillic Romanian texts is a separate skill, and tables matching old Romanian Cyrillic letters to their modern equivalents are worth keeping on hand separately from any tables of Russian or Church Slavonic cursive, since the letters and their sound values don't always correspond.

Stare Civilă: The 1865 Reform and Two Parallel Streams of Documents

Before Cuza's reform, the recording of births, marriages and deaths in the Danubian principalities was handled exclusively by Orthodox priests, in books called 'condici'. The 1865 Civil Code, modelled largely on the French Napoleonic Code, introduced a state system of civil registration (stare civilă) — and from that point on, the Old Kingdom had two independent streams of documentation: church registers, which continued to be kept out of habit for several more decades, and state registers, which became the official legal document. For the period between 1865 and the close of the nineteenth century, it's worth checking both sources, since local parishes didn't always transfer their data to the secular authorities immediately or completely.

Transylvania: Hungarian Chancery and Saxon Lutherans

Transylvania became part of Austria-Hungary long before Romania's unification and remained under Budapest's authority until 1918, so documents from this period are drawn up under Hungarian administrative norms, usually in Hungarian or Latin, even for parishes that were ethnically Romanian and Orthodox or Greek Catholic. Civil registration in the Hungarian part of the empire was introduced in 1895 under a separate law that unified record-keeping across all of Hungary, Transylvania included. A substantial share of these registers was later transferred to regional Romanian archives — the Service Județean al Arhivelor Naționale in Cluj, Sibiu, Târgu Mureș — but part of the originals, and nearly all the pre-war Hungarian state registers, remained in Hungary and are held at the Hungarian National Archives (Magyar Nemzeti Levéltár) in Budapest.

A separate and often overlooked category is the Transylvanian Saxons, a German-speaking Lutheran and Catholic community that settled in the region as early as the twelfth and thirteenth centuries at the invitation of Hungarian kings and maintained its own church record-keeping in German right up until the mass emigration to Germany after 1990. For descendants of Saxon families, the registers are held in Transylvania's Lutheran and Catholic deanery archives, as well as partly in German archives of heritage associations — an entirely separate system from the Orthodox and Greek Catholic Romanian fonds of the same region.

Bessarabia: A Governorate That Became Moldova

The territory between the Prut and Dniester rivers was annexed to the Russian Empire in 1812 following another Russo-Turkish War and was administered as the Bessarabian governorate until 1918, when it joined Romania along with Transylvania and Bukovina. The archival trail of a Bessarabian family depends on the specific period: documents predating 1918 — consistory registers and governorate records — were deposited in Chișinău rather than Bucharest, and today the bulk of them are held at the National Archive of the Republic of Moldova. Documents from the interwar period of 1918–1940 sit partly there as well, and partly in Romanian regional archives, since Bessarabia was officially Romanian territory during those years. After its absorption into the USSR in 1940, the archival system switched again, this time to the Soviet one, and for a family that spent the entire twentieth century in the same village, a register entry might successively turn up in the fonds of three different states.

Northern Bukovina: A Province That Stopped Being Romanian

Austrian Bukovina was administered in a manner similar to neighbouring Galicia and was split in 1940 between Romania and the USSR: the southern part, with the city of Suceava, remained Romanian, while the northern part, with Chernivtsi, became Ukrainian. For families from northern Bukovina, the registers are held today in the Chernivtsi regional archive of Ukraine rather than in Romanian or Austrian fonds, even though the parish itself was administered under Viennese law until 1918. This 1940 border regularly catches descendants off guard: they look for their home village on a modern map of Romania, fail to find it, and don't realise they need to search on the other side of the Ukrainian border.

Who Chose the Child's Name: The Role of Godparents in Romanian Tradition

In the Romanian Orthodox tradition, a child's name was frequently chosen by the godfather (naș) or godmother (nașă) rather than by the parents, and across several generations of the same family a name could be passed on consistently through godparents rather than directly from parents to children — worth bearing in mind when trying to guess an ancestor's name from the 'logic' of family relationships. Boyar (noble) surnames in Moldavia and Wallachia frequently included a toponymic element — the name of an estate or ancestral village — which over time turned into a hereditary surname even after the family had lost the estate itself.

Where to Search Online: The National Archive, FamilySearch and Hungarian Databases

The National Archives of Romania (Arhivele Naționale ale României) run their own portal listing fonds, but unlike Poland's PRADZIAD, they don't offer a single nationwide name-search index — requests have to be sent directly to the regional branch for the place where the event occurred. A substantial share of Romanian church registers from the eighteenth to twentieth centuries, particularly from Transylvania and Moldavia, were microfilmed by the Mormon Genealogical Society of Utah during the Soviet and post-Soviet period and are now viewable on FamilySearch — often faster than corresponding with a regional archive. For Transylvanian fonds, it's worth additionally checking the Hungarian portal Hungaricana and the Magyar Nemzeti Levéltár catalogue, where a substantial share of pre-war registers and censuses has already been digitised and made openly accessible.

Jewish Communities: JewishGen Romania and a Separate Group for Bessarabia

The Jewish population of historical Moldavia, Bukovina and Bessarabia was among the largest in Europe, and for this category of ancestors the principal resource is JewishGen's Romania Research Division, which brings together synagogue registers, censuses and emigration lists for all regions of present-day Romania. For Bessarabia, there's a separate, more narrowly focused structure — the Bessarabia SIG (Special Interest Group), also under JewishGen, which maintains its own database specifically for Bessarabian shtetls and often holds material not included in the general Romanian section. Since a substantial share of the Jewish population left the region in waves of emigration in the late nineteenth and first half of the twentieth century, a register found in a local archive often turns out to be just the first link in a chain that continues into the archives of the United States, Israel or Argentina.

Six Mistakes in Romanian Genealogical Research

  • Searching for a register exclusively through Bucharest, without checking whether the village belonged to Transylvania, Bessarabia or Bukovina, each with its own separate archival system.
  • Mistaking pre-reform Cyrillic entries for a document in an unfamiliar language, when it's actually the same Romanian language, just in a different alphabet.
  • Ignoring Hungarian fonds for Transylvanian ancestors and limiting the search to Romanian regional archives alone.
  • Overlooking the 1940 border change for Bukovina — part of the villages are now physically located in Ukraine.
  • Confusing the general JewishGen Romania section with the more narrowly focused Bessarabia SIG when the matter specifically concerns Bessarabian shtetls.
  • Ignoring the role of godparents in choosing a name, and trying to deduce an ancestor's name purely from the 'logic' of their parents' names.

Search Checklist

  • Establish the precise name of the village or town and determine which of the four historical regions it belonged to at the relevant time.
  • If the region is Transylvania, check both the Romanian regional archive and the Hungarian portals Hungaricana and Magyar Nemzeti Levéltár.
  • If the region is Bessarabia, start your search at the National Archive of Moldova in Chișinău, not in Bucharest.
  • If the region is northern Bukovina, check whether the village now falls within Ukraine's Chernivtsi region.
  • For records predating the 1860s, be prepared for the document to be written in Cyrillic, even though the language is Romanian.
  • Check whether the parish you need has been digitised on FamilySearch before sending a paid request to the archive.
  • For Jewish ancestors, check both the general JewishGen Romania section and, where applicable, the specialised Bessarabia SIG database.

Romanian archival research is complicated not because there are too few documents, but because the country in its modern borders has existed only since 1918, and before that its lands were governed by four different systems — Ottoman autonomy, the Austro-Hungarian chancery, Russian governorate administration, and Romania's own state. Understanding which of these systems was responsible for a particular village in a particular year turns scattered archival fragments into the coherent story of one family.

Glossary

Moldavia and Wallachia — two Danubian principalities under Ottoman suzerainty that united in 1859 and formed the basis of modern Romania.

Stare civilă — the state system of civil status registration, introduced in Romania by the 1865 Civil Code.

Condică — a church register recording births, marriages and deaths in the Orthodox parishes of the Danubian principalities before the introduction of civil registration.

Arhivele Naționale ale României — the National Archives of Romania in Bucharest, with a network of regional branches (Service Județean).

Transylvanian Saxons — a German-speaking Lutheran and Catholic community of Transylvania, settled in the region in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries.

Magyar Nemzeti Levéltár — the Hungarian National Archives in Budapest, holding a substantial share of pre-war Transylvanian registers.

JewishGen Romania Research Division — a specialised database of Jewish vital records and other documents for the historical regions of Romania.

Bessarabia SIG — a separate, narrowly focused group under JewishGen, maintaining a database of Jewish shtetls in historical Bessarabia.
